Chapter Two: The Hole in the Floor

By morning the kitchen had become somewhere else.

Not physically — the lino was still peeling, the fridge still hummed its one note, the radio still found cheerful presenters to fill the silence between adverts for things nobody could afford. But Callum moved through it differently. He sat at the table with Owen’s cereal bowl pushed aside and his own tea forgotten, and he kept seeing the back bedroom window of number nine in the space between his eyelids and the room.

He’d done it. Actually gone inside.

Not far. Just the back porch, really. A square of linoleum curling at the edges, a coat rack with one hook left, a door into the kitchen that he’d not opened. But inside. Over the threshold.

His mother didn’t ask where he’d been. She didn’t ask much of him these days, not since the firing from Romano’s had become official and she’d stopped expecting answers that weren’t disappointing. She slid a plate of toast across the table and sat opposite him with her own, and they ate in the companionable silence of people who had run out of things to say to each other years ago.

Owen was the only one talking. Something about a teacher who’d marked his English homework wrong because he’d used the word “actually” too many times, which Owen thought was unfair because the word was useful and the teacher was a “meanie.”

Callum nodded in the right places.

His phone was on the table beside him. He’d taken four pictures at number nine. The front. The side gate. The back door. The upstairs window from the lane.

He swiped through them now, watching the cream curtains and the cracked path and the overgrown garden. They looked ordinary. That was the worst part. If the house had been boarded up, if there’d been graffiti or broken windows or a “condemned” sign, he could have reported it and left it at that. But it looked lived in, just badly. Just quietly.

The back door photo showed the Yale lock. He’d taken it close. You could see the rot in the frame.

Deborah stood to clear the plates. “Don’t sit here all day.”

“I’m not.”

“You are. You’ve got that face.”

“What face?”

“The face that says you’re ten thousand miles away.”

She went to the sink and ran water too hot, steam fogging the window. Callum looked at his phone again. The figure in the window from last night — he couldn’t see it in any of the pictures, but he remembered the weight of it. The deliberate way it had leaned closer.

He opened a new note and started typing.

Alder Lane properties. Council tax band. Number nine.

He typed fast, almost angry, as if naming the thing would shrink it.

Deborah dried her hands on her apron and came back to the table. “What are you doing?”

“Research.”

“On what?”

“A house.”

“Which house?”

He didn’t answer. She leaned in and read over his shoulder. Her face did something complicated — not quite worry, not quite anger, more like the expression people wear when they realise the thing they’ve been pretending isn’t happening has just stood up and introduced itself.

“That’s number nine,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Don’t.”

“Don’t what?”

“Don’t get involved. It’s none of our business.”

“I’m not involved. I’m curious.”

“Curiosity got your father killed.”

Callum looked at her. She’d never said that before — not exactly. She’d implied it, in the way she said his name sometimes, with a sharp little edge, as if he were a reminder of something she’d tried to put down. But she’d never said it flat out.

“Is that what happened?” he asked.

She picked up Owen’s empty cereal bowl and carried it to the sink without answering.

He went back to his room and opened his laptop. The blue-white light filled half his face again. He typed the address into Land Registry search, then council tax records, then planning applications. Most of it came back blank or paid-access gated. One thing did surface: a probate record from six years ago, a name — M. H. Alderton — and the note “estate in probate, no next of kin located.”

He screenshot it.

The name meant nothing to him. But “no next of kin located” meant something. It meant a house that had emptied out and never refilled, not properly. A house that had sat in the legal system like a stone in a shoe while nobody came to claim it.

He sat back.

The monitor reflected in the window. Behind him, the street. Number nine, cream curtains, blank glass.

He wondered what the inside looked like.

That night he slept badly. Dreamed of doors. Hundreds of doors, all different sizes, all slightly ajar. He was trying to push through one particular door that wouldn’t open no matter how hard he pressed, and behind it he could hear breathing.

When he woke at six, the dream stayed with him. The smell of the back garden was still in his nose — damp, earth, chemicals.

He went downstairs. Deborah was already in her uniform, coffee in a travel mug. Owen was watching cartoons with the volume too low.

“I’m going out later,” Callum said.

“Where?”

“Just out.”

“Take Owen.”

“He’s got school.”

“It’s Saturday.”

“Oh. Right.”

She looked at him for a long time. “Be careful.”

“I’m always careful.”

“No,” she said. “You’re not.”

She left at seven. Owen went to his room to play on his tablet. Callum waited twenty minutes, then put on his coat and stepped outside.

The lane was quiet. A cat moved along a garden wall like it owned the street. A curtain twitched at number eleven — the woman who worked nights, probably asleep by now.

Callum walked to number nine and stood in front of it.

He didn’t go in. Not this time.

He just stood there, letting the house look at him, and after a while he thought he could feel it doing exactly that. Not watching from a window. Just being. A presence inside walls that had grown used to their own silence.

He took one more picture of the front door.

Then he turned and walked home.
