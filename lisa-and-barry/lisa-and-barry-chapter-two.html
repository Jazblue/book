Lisa and Barry

Chapter Two: The Changing Room

The pool had been Lisa’s idea. Now Barry had to face the water, the trunks, and the quiet humiliation of being seen.

On Wednesday morning, Lisa arrived wearing a yellow dress that was too bright for September. She had her hair in a ponytail. She was carrying a black sports bag.

"Swimming," she said, like he might have forgotten.

He had not forgotten. She had mentioned it last week, while she was chopping onions in his kitchen. She had said the pool was quiet on Wednesday mornings. She had said it would be good for his joints. She had said, "I'll take you," like it was the simplest thing in the world.

It was not the simplest thing. The simplest thing was tea. The simplest thing was toast. The simplest thing was sitting in the armchair by the window and listening to the rain. Swimming required trunks. Swimming required changing rooms. Swimming required being looked at.

He had not been swimming since he was a boy. His father had taken him once, to a lido that no longer existed. He remembered the smell of chlorine and the slap of water and the way his father had held him under the surface for three seconds too many. He had not been back since.

"I bought you something," Lisa said. She opened her bag. She pulled out a pair of swim trunks. Blue. patterned with anchors. "I hope they fit. I guessed at the size."

"How much?" he asked.

"Don't worry about it."

"Lisa."

"My money. My choice. You need them."

He took them. The fabric felt thick. Too thick. He ran his fingers along the waistband. "They feel small."

"They'll stretch."

"I can't stretch them."

She laughed. "You'll be fine."

He was not fine. He spent the morning listening to the clock on the wall. It was an old clock, the kind with a pendulum that swung and a chime that rang every fifteen minutes. It had been his grandmother's. It was the only clock he had ever needed. He did not look at watches. He did not look at phone screens. He listened to the quarter-hours and they told him where he was in the day.

At eleven, the clock chimed. Lisa was six minutes late. Six minutes was not unusual. She was always a few minutes late. She said it was because the bus was unreliable. He thought it was because she liked making him wait. Not in a cruel way. In a way that made the arrival feel like an event.

At eleven oh-six, the intercom rang. The usual ring: two short bursts, a pause, then a longer buzz. He shifted in his armchair. He felt his way to the hallway, his hand trailing along the wall, counting the doorframes, finding the stairs by the change in air pressure. He lived on the ground floor. The flat was small — one bedroom, one bathroom, a kitchen that opened onto a living room. He liked it small. Small was knowable. Small was safe.

He pressed the button. He unlocked the door. He heard her footsteps on the path, then on the hall tiles, then on the stair carpet that was not there because he was on the ground floor.

"Hello, Barry," she said. Her voice was warm. Her footsteps had the particular bounce he had come to listen for.

"Hello."

She came in. She was wearing trainers. He could hear them squeak. She was wearing a coat that rustled. She was wearing the yellow dress under it. He knew because she had told him, and because he could smell something floral — lavender, maybe, or soap — that was not the coat.

"Are you ready?" she asked.

He had his blind stick by the door. He had showered an hour ago, scrubbing his skin until it was pink, as though cleanliness could wash away the nervousness. He had put on jogging bottoms and a t-shirt. It was still warm — early September, the kind of heat that sits in the bricks and the pavement and does not know it should leave.

"I'm ready."

She took his arm. She led him out. The walk to the pool was fifteen minutes. She talked while they walked. She talked about her mother. She talked about her studies. She talked about a film she had seen the night before. She did not talk about Mark. She had not mentioned Mark for three days. Barry did not ask why.

The pool was in a leisure centre on the high street. It was quiet, as she had promised. There were only a handful of people in the water. The air smelled of chlorine and rubber. The changing rooms were single-sex, but the carer's key allowed Lisa to enter with him. They found a cubicle at the far end, away from the main entrance. It was narrow. The benches were hard. There was a hook on the wall. There was a shower head that leaked.

Lisa closed the curtain. She was matter-of-fact. She did not blush. She did not look away. She pulled her dress over her head in one smooth movement and folded it on the bench. Barry heard the rustle. He heard the soft sound of fabric. He heard her move in the small space. He stood very still. He held his stick in front of him like a shield.

"You can put those here," she said, pointing to the bench.

He took off his t-shirt. He folded it. He pulled down his jogging bottoms. He stood in his underwear — plain, grey, the kind that had seen better decades. He felt the air on his skin. He felt exposed. He had not been undressed in front of anyone since he was a boy. Not even in the bath, not really — she would leave the room, close the door, give him the dignity of steam and silence.

"Do you want a hand?" she asked.

"No."

He pulled the trunks on. They were tight. Too tight. The fabric pressed against him in ways that were not comfortable. He adjusted the waistband. He adjusted again. He felt like he was wearing a costume that did not belong to him.

"They'll loosen up," she said. She was already in her swimsuit. He knew because she told him. He could hear the change in her voice — higher, lighter, the way people sound when they are in water clothes. "It's just a blue one-piece. Nothing fancy."

"What are you wearing?" he asked.

"Just a swimsuit."

He laughed. It was a nervous sound. "I can hear it. The material. It sounds different."

"You can touch it, if you want."

He stopped. The offer hung in the air between them, small and enormous. He reached out. His hand found her shoulder. The fabric was smooth, cool, tight against her skin. He ran his fingers along the seam. It was strange — touching someone through water clothes was different from touching someone through everyday clothes. There was no give. There was no softness. There was only the shape of her underneath, hard and definite and real.

"Okay?" she asked.

"Yeah."

He pulled his hand back. He felt his cheeks warm. He had touched her arm before, her hand, the small of her back when he had stumbled on the stairs. This was different. This was intimate. This was the kind of touch that belonged to people who were allowed to see each other.

"You can try again," she said. She was not mocking him. She was patient. She was always patient.

He reached out again. This time his hand found her upper arm. The muscle was firm. The fabric was wet already, or maybe it was just cold from the changing room air. He ran his fingers up to her shoulder, along the strap of the swimsuit, down to the curve of her back. She did not move. She stood still while he explored, like she was a statue he was allowed to touch.

Then they were in the water.

The pool was warmer than he expected. The water rose to his chest. He held the side with one hand, his stick in the other. He did not let go of the stick. He had never needed it in water before. He felt ridiculous. He felt like a child holding a parent's hand.

"You can put it down," Lisa said. She was beside him, treading water, her voice bright. "The bottom is only two metres. You can stand."

He put the stick on the edge. He let go of the wall. He stood. The water held him. For a moment, he was weightless. For a moment, he was not a blind man in a public pool on a Wednesday morning. He was just a man in water.

They swam. Or rather, Lisa swam and Barry paddled. He kicked his legs. He moved his arms. He kept his head above water. He felt clumsy. He felt slow. He felt like he was doing it wrong.

But Lisa was with him. She stayed close. She corrected his stroke without making him feel stupid. She held his hand when he drifted too far from the wall. She laughed when he swallowed water and spat it out.

And there was touching. Lots of it. In the water, touch was different. The water made everything softer, everything slower. Her hand on his shoulder to guide him. Her hand on his chest to steady him. Her leg brushing his leg when they turned. Her arm around his waist when he panicked, just for a second, when the water rose over his head and the world went silent and weightless.

He was sure, more than once, that he felt her soft bosom against his arm. It was brief. It was accidental. It was the kind of contact that happens when two people are close in water and gravity does not matter. Each time, he pretended he had not noticed. Each time, he felt a warmth that was not from the pool.

He kept touching his trunks. They were tight. They were uncomfortable. They were a constant reminder that he was undressed in front of someone, that his body was on display, that he was not the man he used to be. He pulled at the waistband. He shifted. He adjusted.

"I can get a bigger size," Lisa said. She was treading water beside him, smiling. "For a bigger man."

He laughed. It surprised him. "You're joking."

"Maybe. Maybe not."

"You're impossible."

"I'm honest."

They both laughed. The sound echoed off the tiles. For a moment, the pool was theirs. The other swimmers were distant. The clock on the wall was far away. The world outside the water did not exist.

Later, they had coffee in the cafeteria upstairs. The pool was below them. The water smelt faintly through the floorboards. The cafeteria was almost empty — just an old man in the corner and a woman with a pushchair. It was very quiet for a Wednesday morning. Lisa said so.

"Where is everyone?" she asked, stirring sugar into her latte.

"At work," Barry said. "It's eleven thirty. People are at work."

"I suppose."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "You were good in there. Better than I thought you'd be."

"I swallowed a litre of pool water."

"That's normal."

He stirred his tea. He had not drunk coffee yet. He had not had coffee since he was a teenager. He did not like things that made him jittery. He liked things that were steady, things that lasted, things that did not rush.

"Can I ask you something?" Lisa said.

"Anything."

"Have you ever... been with anyone?"

The question landed softly, like a leaf. He had expected it. He had been waiting for it, in a way. The pool, the changing room, the closeness — it had been leading somewhere.

"No," he said.

"Never?"

"Never."

"Not even...?"

"Not even."

She was quiet. He could hear her breathing. She was thinking. He knew the sound of her thinking — a small hum, almost imperceptible, like a fridge in another room.

"You don't have to be embarrassed," she said. "There are sites. Specialised ones, for... for people who are blind or have other needs. You can describe what you want. You can match with someone who understands. You don't have to do it alone."

He laughed. It was a short, dry sound. "I don't want a site. I don't want a stranger."

"Then what do you want?"

He did not answer. He could not answer. What he wanted was standing across from him in a yellow dress, holding a latte, with her hair in a ponytail and her feet in trainers that squeaked. What he wanted was the sound of her voice in the morning. What he wanted was the way she laughed at his jokes, even when they were not funny. What he wanted was not for strangers on a screen.

"I don't know," he said.

"That's okay."

She reached across the table. She touched his hand. Her fingers were warm. Her nails were painted pink. She had done it the night before, he knew — she had told him she was painting her nails while watching television. He had pictured it: her on his sofa, her legs up on the arm, the television flickering, the smell of nail polish in the flat.

"I meant what I said," she said. "About the trunks. About the swimming. About being here."

"I know."

"And... about other things, too. If you ever want to talk. Or... do anything. I'm not going anywhere."

He squeezed her hand. It was the first time he had initiated touch. It was the first time he had reached out without being prompted. It felt brave. It felt stupid. It felt like standing on the edge of a cliff and deciding to jump.

She did not pull away. She held his hand. The café was quiet. The old man in the corner turned a page of his newspaper. The woman with the pushchair rocked it gently. Outside, the high street murmured with traffic.

"I want a younger model," Barry said. It was a joke. It was a way to lighten the moment. "With big tits. Like yours."

She did not laugh. She did not reply. She let go of his hand. She stirred her latte again. The silence between them stretched, thin and tight, like the fabric of his trunks.

"I should go," she said.

"Lisa —"

"Mark," she said. She said it quietly. "He asked where I was last night. He called twice."

"Oh."

"I didn't answer."

She stood. She paid for the coffees. She led him back to the pool, then to the changing room, then out into the September air. On the walk back to the flat, she was quiet. She held his arm, but she did not speak. She did not squeeze it. She did not bounce. She was somewhere else.

When they reached the flat, she unlocked the door. She hung her coat. She took off her trainers. She stood in the hallway, unsure, like she did not know whether to stay or leave.

"Do you want a cuppa?" Barry asked.

She hesitated. Then: "I should go home, Barry."

"Okay."

"I'll see you Friday."

"Friday."

She kissed his cheek. It was quick, light, almost accidental — the same as before. Then she was gone. The door closed softly. The lock clicked. The hallway was empty.

Barry stood in the hallway and listened to the clock. It chimed twelve. The quarters ticked by. The flat was quiet. The clock was loud. He thought about the pool. He thought about the trunks. He thought about the touch — her hand on his, her arm in the water, the brief warmth of her bosom against his skin. He thought about Mark and the phone calls and the silence on the walk home.

He thought: *I am a virgin.*

He thought: *She knows.*

He thought: *She offered.*

He did not know what to do with any of it. He stood by the door and let the clock tell him where he was. Fifteen minutes past. Thirty. Forty-five. The hour.

He sat in his armchair. He listened to the rain return. He listened to the building settle. He listened to the clock, and for the first time in a long time, he did not find it comforting.

Somewhere above him, the couple from last week were quiet. The bed did not creak. The walls did not hum. The world was still.

Barry sat in the dark and thought about trunks that were too small and a hand that had held his and a woman who was not his and not anyone else's either, not really, not in the way that mattered.

He thought: *Friday is a long time away.*

He thought: *I will count the quarters until she comes back.*
