Deborah found the screwdriver on Sunday afternoon.

Callum had left it on the kitchen table by mistake. He had used it at number nine — the back door had been stubborn, the lock swollen with damp — and he had set it down on the step while he checked the handle, then forgotten it. He had gone back for it, but Deborah had found it first.

She held it up when he came in. “Where did you get this?”

“Kitchen drawer,” Callum said.

“It wasn’t in the kitchen drawer. It was mine.”

Callum said nothing. He had taken it from the drawer. It had been in the drawer. Deborah was lying, or she had moved it, or she had simply decided that it had never been there.

“I’m going to keep this,” she said. “You can’t go around opening doors that aren’t yours.”

“It’s not for opening doors.”

“Then what is it for?”

Callum looked at the floor. The linoleum was scuffed near the fridge, a white mark he had made when he was younger and carrying something heavy. He remembered the day. He had been carrying Owen’s buggy down the step and slipped. Deborah had shouted at him for being careless. She had not asked if he was hurt.

“I need it,” he said.

“For what?”

He did not answer. He could not say: I need it because number nine has a lock on the front door that won’t turn, and I need to see what’s inside. He could not say: I need it because there is something in that house that is not leaving, and I need to know whether it is the kind of thing that can be kept out with a piece of metal and a twist of the wrist.

Deborah put the screwdriver in the drawer and pushed it shut. “No more,” she said. “Understand?”

Callum understood.

He went upstairs. He stood in the hallway for a moment, listening to Owen in the living room, to Deborah in the kitchen. Then he went into his room and closed the door.

He did not lock it. There was no point. She would only try the handle.

Instead he sat on the floor with his back against the wardrobe and thought about the front door of number nine. The one he had never tried. The one that faced Alder Lane, that faced the street, that faced the houses opposite and the people who might be watching.

He had a key. Not the right key. A screwdriver was not a key. But it was something. It was a way in.

That night, he dreamt of the front door of number nine opening. Not because he had opened it. Because it had opened by itself. And on the step, in the light from the street, stood a woman. She was holding a towel. She was wet. She was wearing a look on her face that said: I have been waiting for you to find me.

He woke up before the dream finished.

The room was dark. Owen was breathing through the wall. Somewhere outside, a car passed. Callum lay still and listened to the house settle around him. He thought about the bathroom door and the lock he had installed. He thought about Deborah’s hand on the handle. He thought about the screwdriver in the kitchen drawer, out of reach.

He put his hand under his pillow.

The key he had taken from the shed was still there. A small, cold weight. He had found it in the shed on the day he discovered the chair, tucked into a hole in the wall behind the blankets. He had not told anyone. He had not told Deborah. He had not told Owen.

Now he held it in the dark.

He turned it over in his fingers. It was not the key to number nine. It was not the key to his own house. It was something else. A door somewhere, waiting to be opened.

Callum slipped out of bed. He put on his shoes in the hall. He unlocked the back door and stepped into the garden. The grass was cold on his feet. Number nine was across the road, dark and still.

He walked to the front of the house, to the gate, and looked down Alder Lane. The street was empty. The lamps were on. The air smelled of cut grass and the neighbour’s cat, which was not there.

He thought about the front door of number nine. He thought about the woman in his dream. He thought about Deborah, asleep upstairs, with the screwdriver in the kitchen drawer and the lock on the bathroom door that she had watched him install.

He had a key now. Not the right one. But a key all the same.

And a house across the road that was not empty, and not abandoned, and not waiting any longer.

Callum went back inside. He locked the back door. He climbed the stairs quietly. He lay down in the dark with the key under his pillow and listened to Owen breathe, and for the first time in weeks, he did not think about the figure at the window.

He thought about the door.

He thought about opening it.
