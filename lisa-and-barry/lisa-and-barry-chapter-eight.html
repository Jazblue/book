Lisa and Barry

Chapter Eight: Two Women on Wednesday

The schedule changed. Wednesday became the only day. And then came two words he did not understand: Netflix and chill.

He tried to ring Shelly on Monday morning. The app connected. The number rang. He counted the tones — one, two, three — before the voicemail clicked in. He left a message. He told her he was sorry for the delay. He told her he was looking forward to Wednesday. He told her he would bring the coffee this time.

He did not hear back.

On Tuesday, he tried again. Same result. The phone rang. The voicemail swallowed his voice. He left another message, shorter this time, less apologetic, more steady. He did not want to beg. He did not want to seem desperate. But he was, a little. He wanted the date. He wanted something normal. He wanted a woman who would answer his calls and drink coffee with him and not leave him with an erection and a shopping list wrapped around a condom in his pocket.

Wednesday came at eleven oh-six. Lisa was exactly on time. No delay. No bus excuse. No bounce in her footsteps, exactly, but something close — a lightness, a quickness, like she had been looking forward to this.

"Hello, Barry," she said.

"Hello."

She came in. She was not wearing a coat. She was wearing a t-shirt and shorts, like it was summer, like the flat was warm, like she had planned to stay. She hung her bag on the hook by the door. The same bag — the one from Chapter Three, the one with the nightie and the knickers and the tampons, the one he had opened, the one she had not taken back.

"You can't change my Wednesday," Barry said. It came out firmer than he intended. "I want to see Woody Woodpecker."

Lisa laughed. It was bright, immediate, the kind of laugh that filled the hallway. "Come on," she said. "No games."

"We're going swimming later, then."

"It's your fault," she said. She stepped inside. She closed the door. He heard the lock click. "It's my only day off, and I want to see you."

"Friday?"

"Friday's off. I've taken more hours at the care home. I told the charity. I'm needed Fridays now."

Barry frowned. "Since when?"

"Since Monday. They called. I said yes."

"You didn't tell me."

"I know."

She moved past him into the living room. He heard the cushion dip. He followed, his hand trailing along the wall. He sat in the armchair. The flat felt different — not warmer, exactly, but tighter, as though the space between them had shrunk.

"Perhaps your Shelly wouldn't mind getting on cleaning gear for you," Lisa said. Her voice was light, teasing, but there was an edge to it — the Lisa switch, he thought, the one that could turn playful into sharp in half a second.

"Shelly's not my cleaner," he said.

"No?"

"No."

"Pity. Sounds like she needs the work."

He did not reply. He thought about the missed calls, the voicemails, the silence on the other end of the line. He thought about Wednesday and the date he had arranged and the woman who had said she would bring coffee and the woman who had not answered.

"How's the condom?" Lisa asked.

Barry froze. His face warmed. He had not told her. He had not told anyone. The condom was still in his pocket, the shopping list wrapped around it, the paper crinkled from being carried around for three days. He had not thrown it away. He had not used it again. He had simply carried it, like a secret, like a proof of something he could not name.

"What?" he said.

"The condom. From the bag. Did you use it?"

"I... yes."

"Felt good?"

"Lisa —"

"I'm serious. Did it feel good?"

He was silent. The question was direct. The tone was matter-of-fact, the same tone she used when she asked if he wanted more garlic or if the bath was too hot. She was not embarrassed. She was not shy. She wanted to know.

"Yes," he said eventually.

"Good. That's what I want to discover with you."

The words landed softly, like stones in water. Barry did not know how to respond. He wanted to say: *What does that mean?* He wanted to say: *I don't know if I can do this again.* He wanted to say: *I called Shelly. I arranged a date. I want something real.* Instead, he said: "Honestly?"

"Honestly what?"

"I'm interested in a relationship first."

The silence that followed was not empty. It was full — full of her shifting on the sofa, full of the clock ticking, full of the hum of the fridge, full of the things neither of them was saying. Barry sat with his hands in his lap and waited for her to laugh, to tease, to brush it off with a joke. She did not. She was quiet for a long time.

"Okay," she said eventually. "Mr Swimming. And that's it, then."

Her voice was flat. Resigned. The edge was back, but different now — not sharp, not angry, but distant, like she was already packing her bag and walking out the door.

"Lisa —"

"I'll take my bag back."

She stood. Barry heard her move to the hallway. He heard the zip of the bag — the same bag, the one she had left three weeks ago, the one he had opened, the one that contained the condom he had used and the underwear he had tried on and the secret he had kept from her. He heard her lift it off the floor. He heard the strap creak.

"Maybe stay over," he said. The words came out before he could stop them. They sounded desperate. They were desperate. He did not want her to go. He did not want the flat to be empty again. He did not want the silence to return.

She paused. The bag was in her hand. The hallway was quiet.

"But everyone has needs, right?" she said.

Her voice was different now. Softer. Warier. The Lisa switch had flipped again — from distant to something else, something tentative, something that wanted to believe him but did not know how.

"Netflix and chill," she said, almost like a question.

"What's that?" Barry asked.

"Well... I watch Netflix. And you chill."

"Stay in?"

"Forget the swimming..."

He thought about the trunks. He thought about the changing room. He thought about the water and the smell of chlorine and the way she had guided his hand in the pool. He thought about Wednesday as a swimming day — the routine, the timing, the special day they had claimed from the chaos of her week.

"That sounds like a plan," he said.

She was quiet for a moment. Then he heard the bag set down. The strap hit the floor with a soft thud. He heard her move back to the living room. He heard the cushion dip — not on the armchair, but on the sofa, closer to him than usual.

"I'll stay over," she said. "And cook. You're in for a treat."

He smiled. "Two women on Wednesday," he said, trying for lightness. "What could be better?"

Lisa laughed — a real laugh, warm and unguarded. "You're terrible," she said.

"I know."

"Shelly would kill you."

"Shelly doesn't know about me."

"She will."

The clock ticked. The morning light came through the window, thin and grey. The flat was quiet. Lisa shifted on the sofa. She turned towards him. He felt the warmth of her arm through the fabric of her t-shirt.

"Wednesday, then," she said.

"Wednesday."

She did not leave immediately. She stayed. She made toast. She burnt it slightly. She laughed about it. She tidied the kitchen. She moved with a purpose that was not the cleaning purpose of Fridays, but something softer — the purpose of someone who is settling in, who is making herself at home, who is deciding, slowly, that she might stay.

When she finally left, at two, she kissed his cheek — quick, light, the same as always — but her hand lingered on his face, her fingers warm on his jaw.

"Wednesday," she said again.

"Wednesday."

Barry stood in the hallway and listened to the lock click. He listened to her footsteps on the path. He listened to the silence that followed. He thought about the bag — still in the hallway, still half-open, still full of her things. He thought about the condom in his pocket, the shopping list crinkled around it. He thought about Shelly, who had not called back, who might never call back, who had been forgotten again.

He thought: *I am in trouble.*

He thought: *I don't care.*

He thought: *Wednesday cannot come soon enough.*

The clock chimed two fifteen. The afternoon stretched ahead of him, long and empty and full of the smell of burnt toast and lavender and the possibility of something new.
