Chapter Eleven: The House Across the Road

The key was smaller than he expected.

The key was smaller than he expected.

He had found it in the shed, tucked into a hole behind the blankets where the old chair sat. It was brass, dented at the bow, the teeth worn smooth. It did not look like it belonged to anything modern. It looked like it belonged to something that had been locked a long time ago and not opened since.

Callum stood at the back door of his own house and held it in his palm. The key to number nine — if it was the key to number nine — was in his hand. He had spent weeks watching the house. Watching the figure at the upper window. Watching the door that would not stay shut. Watching the shed and the chair and the hole in the wall. He had imagined this moment in a hundred different ways. None of them had included the key being so small.

Owen was asleep. Deborah was asleep. The house was quiet in the way houses are at three in the morning, when the pipes settle and the floorboards remember the day’s footsteps and replay them softly in the dark. Callum pulled on his shoes in the hallway. He did not bother with socks. The carpet was thin and the floorboards were cold but he did not care about cold.

He unlocked the back door. The latch clicked louder than he had anticipated. He paused, listening. Nothing moved. The house breathed around him, indifferent. He stepped outside.

The grass was wet. Not wet from rain — not exactly. The grass on Alder Lane was always damp, as though the earth beneath it sweated something slow and thin. Callum wove between the cars on the drive and out to the pavement. The street was empty. The lamps were on, each one casting a small, sickly pool of yellow onto the wet tarmac. Number nine was across the road and two doors down. It sat in the dark between lamps, the front garden a blacker patch than the pavement around it.

Callum crossed.

The gate at number nine was loose. It swung a little when he pressed it, the hinge making a sound like a breath through teeth. He went up the path. The front door was there. He had looked at it so many times from his own window that he could have drawn it from memory — paint the colour of tea, a letter flap with the flap missing, a number nine that had never been properly wiped clean.

He held up the key.

It did not fit the lock.

He turned it gently. Then with more pressure. Then not at all — he simply pressed it against the keyhole and the bow of the key flexed slightly and he knew. It was not the key to the front door. It never had been.

He lowered his hand. The key was warm from his palm.

The house was not silent.

It was a small sound, filtered through the walls — a creak from upstairs, the kind a floorboard makes when weight shifts onto it slowly. Callum went very still. He listened. The creak came again, and with it something else: a movement in the curtain at the upper window. He had seen that curtain before. It had not moved then. It was moving now.

He stepped back from the door.

The front gate creaked behind him.

He turned. There was a woman standing on the pavement. She was wearing a coat over something that looked like a nightdress and she was barefoot on the cold tarmac. She was older than him — not by much, four, five years — and she had the kind of face that looked like it had been awake for a long time. Her hair was dark and knotted at the back. There was bruising on her jaw.

“You’re the boy from across the road,” she said.

Callum did not answer. He could not. His throat had closed the way throats do when you are found doing something you should not be doing, even when you are not entirely sure what that thing is.

“I’ve seen you,” she said. “You stand at your window and you look at this house. You think it’s empty.”

Callum found his voice. It came out smaller than he wanted. “Is it?”

She smiled. It was not a warm smile. It was the smile of somebody who had been alone for a while and was not sure how to do it properly anymore. “Depends what you mean by empty.”

The curtain twitched again at the upper window. Neither of them looked.

“I left my boyfriend,” she said, as though this explained everything. It almost did. “He was in Colchester. I walked out. Got on a bus. Came here. This house was empty — the back door was open, the windows were all dark. I thought it was perfect. I was wrong.”

“Wrong how?”

“It’s not empty,” she said. “Not the way I thought.”

Callum looked at the front door. He looked at the key in his hand. He looked at her bare feet on the pavement, blue with cold. “You live here.”

“I do now.”

“You broke in.”

“I walked in. Same thing.”

There was a silence then. The kind of silence that is not really silence at all but the sound of something waiting. A car passed at the far end of the road. Its headlights swept across them briefly — the woman’s face, the bruise on her jaw, the key in Callum’s hand — and then the road was dark again and they were standing on either side of a gate that creaked when the wind touched it.

“You should go home,” she said. But she did not say it like she wanted him to. She said it like she was warning him.

“I had a key,” Callum said.

She looked at the key. She did not reach for it. “That key doesn’t open anything here. I checked. The locks are different.”

“How do you know what key it is?”

“I found one like it,” she said. “In the shed. Behind the blankets. Same dent in the bow.”

Callum’s skin went cold. He had not told anyone about the shed. He had not told Deborah. He had not told Owen. He had not told anyone at all.

“You’ve been in the shed,” he said.

“I’ve been in the house,” she said. “The shed’s part of it. I found the key there on the first night. Put it on the mantelpiece. It’s still there.”

“Why?”

“Because I knew somebody would come looking for it.” She paused. “I didn’t know it would be you, though.”

Callum looked at the upper window again. The curtain was still. “What’s in that room?”

The woman’s face changed. Not much — just the tightening around the eyes, the slight set of the jaw. The way people look when they have thought about something a great deal and have not liked any of the conclusions they reached.

“I haven’t opened it,” she said.

“Why not?”

“Because the door at the top of the stairs is locked. And there’s something behind it. I can hear it. Moving. At night. When the house gets quiet.”

Callum looked at number nine. He looked at the front door and the missing letter flap and the darkness inside that was darker than darkness had any right to be. He thought about the shed. He thought about the chair. He thought about the woman in his dream standing on the step, holding a towel, saying she had been waiting.

This woman was not the woman in his dream.

But she was here. And she was real. And she was looking at him the way people look at things they are not sure they believe in.

“You should go,” she said again. “This isn’t your house. And whatever you think is inside it — whatever you’ve been looking for — it isn’t what you think.”

“How do you know what I’m looking for?”

“I’ve seen you,” she said. “You come to the window at the same time every night. You look at the house. You look at the room upstairs. You’ve been doing it for weeks. You’re not looking for anything. You’re waiting for it to look back.”

She was right.

Callum stood on the pavement and held the key and said nothing. The wind came off the fields at the end of the lane and moved through the hedges and brought the smell of something burning — distant, sweet, the kind of smell that could be a bonfire or something else entirely. The woman wrapped her coat tighter around herself.

“What’s your name?” he said.

“Megan.”

“How long have you been here?”

“Four days.”

“And you haven’t opened the door at the top of the stairs.”

“I told you. I can hear it.”

“What does it sound like?”

Megan looked at him for a long time. Then she looked at the house behind her. Then she looked back at Callum and he saw that she was afraid — not the way Deborah was afraid, which was a sharp, practical fear of things that could be managed or argued with or controlled. This was older. This was the fear of people who had seen things that did not make sense and had not been able to close the door fast enough.

“It sounds,” she said quietly, “like someone breathing.”

Callum looked at the upper window. The curtain moved. Not from the wind. There was no wind. It moved the way curtains move when someone stands behind them and then steps away.

He looked back at Megan. She was not looking at the window. She was looking at him.

“Go home, Callum,” she said. She had not told him her name was Megan. She had not told him anything he had not already worked out. But she knew his name.

“How do you know my name?”

Megan did not answer. She turned and walked back up the path to number nine. The gate creaked as she pushed it open. She did not look back. The front door closed behind her with a sound that was almost — but not quite — a sigh.

Callum stood on the pavement for a long time after she had gone.

The key was still in his hand. It was cold now. He turned it over and looked at the dent in the bow, the worn teeth, the smooth shoulder where the metal had been rubbed by a thumb over years. He thought about the shed. He thought about the chair. He thought about the hole in the wall and the blankets and the way the light had fallen through the gaps in the fence that afternoon he had found it.

He thought about the woman in his dream. The one on the step. The one holding the towel. The one who had said she had been waiting.

Megan was not the woman in his dream.

But Megan was inside the house the woman had been standing in front of.

And the door at the top of the stairs was locked.

And something was breathing behind it.

Callum put the key in his pocket. He turned and walked back across the road. The lamps flickered once as he passed under them — not a power cut, not exactly. Just a shiver in the circuit, a brief hesitation in the current. The street went dark for a second and then the light returned.

He did not stop. He did not look back at number nine. He went up his own path and unlocked the back door and climbed the stairs quietly, the way he had learned to do when Owen was small and a creak could mean an hour of soothing.

He lay down in the dark with the key under his pillow.

He listened to Owen breathe through the wall.

And then, very softly, he heard something else.

A sound from the garden. A footstep on the grass. Not Deborah’s footsteps — Deborah’s footsteps were heavier, more certain. These were light. Deliberate. Someone standing on the lawn outside his window, just at the edge of hearing, not doing anything but standing.

Callum lay still.

He did not move.

The key under his pillow was warm again. As though someone had been holding it.
