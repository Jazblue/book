Chapter Nine: The Water Running Cold

The shower was on a timer.

The shower was on a timer. Callum had not known this until Deborah told him, standing in the doorway of the bathroom while he was still in his pyjamas, her face set in the way it had when she was about to say something she thought was reasonable.

“I’ve put a timer on the boiler,” she said. “Ten minutes. You can’t stay in there all night.”

Callum was holding his toothbrush. “I don’t stay in there all night.”

“You take long enough. The hot water runs out. Owen needs it after you.”

He brushed his teeth without answering. Deborah watched him. She did not leave until he had rinsed and set the brush down on the side of the sink. Then she reached past him and turned the tap off.

“Don’t leave it dripping,” she said.

He heard her on the stairs as he undressed for bed. He heard her in the kitchen, moving plates from the table to the drainer. He heard the television come on in the living room. The house was full of sounds that said: I am here. I am in control. I know what you are doing.

He lay in bed for a long time. The dark was different in the summer. It came later, and it came lighter, so that the room never went fully black. He could see the outline of the wardrobe, the handle of the door, the glow from the streetlamp outside coming through the curtains.

He thought about the shower at number nine. If the house had water. If anyone had been living there long enough to turn the tap on. He had seen the bathroom from the landing — mould in the shower, a toothbrush in a mug. Someone had been there recently. Someone who had used the shower, and left, and not come back.

Or maybe they had come back.

At midnight, Callum got up.

He dressed in the dark. Socks, jeans, jumper. Shoes by the front door, which he put on silently on the step. The lock on the back door turned without a sound. He crossed the road in his socks because the tarmac was still warm from the day, and stood at the back gate of number nine.

The garden smelled the same. Damp, and something older. The shed door was still hanging off its hinge. The washing line posts were still there, rusted.

He went to the back door.

It was locked. He had not expected it to be unlocked. He had the screwdriver in his pocket, but he did not use it. Instead he stood and looked up at the back bedroom window. The curtain was drawn. No shape behind it. No movement.

He waited for ten minutes. Then he turned the handle of the back door, just to see.

It opened.

He had not locked it properly when he left. The lock was so rotten that turning the key did nothing. He had closed it behind him out of habit, not because it would hold.

Callum stepped inside.

The house was colder than the last time. The fridge hummed. The kitchen was the same, but the mug from the coffee table was gone. He noticed this immediately. It had been there before. Now it was not.

He went through to the living room. The sofa sheet was still there. The television was still dark. But on the floor beside the armchair, where there had been nothing before, there was a pair of shoes. Men’s shoes, size ten or eleven, laces undone.

Callum stood very still.

He could hear the pipes now. Not dripping. Running. From upstairs.

He went to the stairs. Climbed them slowly. The runner was still worn through in the centre. The landing smelled of damp carpet and something else — soap, maybe. Shampoo. The sort you buy in a bottle with a pump.

He tried the left door. Bedroom. Same as before. Empty coat. Book with no title. Glass with a dried ring. But the bed had been made. Not well. Just pulled straight. As if someone had lain on it and then decided to leave before finishing.

The right door. Bathroom.

The light was on.

Callum stood in the doorway and looked in.

The shower curtain was drawn. The tap was dripping. And on the side of the bath, where there had been nothing before, there was a bar of soap. Unwrapped. Pale yellow. Almost new.

He did not step inside.

He stood in the doorway and listened. The house was very quiet, but not the quiet of emptiness. It was the quiet of a room someone has just left — the silence that holds the shape of their voice, the echo of their movement in the air.

He went back downstairs. Out the back door. Locked it from the outside, as much as the lock would allow, and crossed the road.

Deborah was still awake. He could hear the television through the wall. He climbed the stairs quietly, took off his shoes in the hall, and went to his room.

He locked the bathroom door in the morning, when he went to wash. Deborah knocked once, firmly. Then she went away.

Callum stood in the shower with the water running cold over his shoulders and thought about number nine. About the bar of soap. About the shoes. About the house that had not been waiting for him to come close enough to hear.

It had been waiting for him to notice that it was no longer empty.
