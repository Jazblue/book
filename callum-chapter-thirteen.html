Callum

Chapter Thirteen: The Breathing

It started on a Wednesday, which Megan thought was appropriate.

It started on a Wednesday, which Megan thought was appropriate. Midweek. The day the week starts to tilt. Nothing important ever happened on a Wednesday. You could believe that, on a Wednesday.

She was in the kitchen. The tins she had found on the second day were still mostly edible — baked beans, soup, something that might have been tomatoes once. She had eaten standing up, the way she had learned to eat in the flat in Colchester when Daniel was in one of his moods and sitting down felt like an invitation for him to sit opposite her and talk. Talking with Daniel was worse than silence. Silence at least left you alone inside your own head.

She was scraping the tin with a spoon when she heard it.

A sound from upstairs.

Not a creak. Not a floorboard settling or a pipe contracting or the house doing the things houses do when they are old and tired of holding themselves up. This was different. It was a wet, soft sound. The kind of sound a mouth makes when it is breathing through it rather than the nose. The kind of sound you might hear if you pressed your ear to something that was alive.

Megan stopped scraping. She held the spoon in mid-air and listened.

The sound came again. Fainter this time, or perhaps just further away. It had moved — she was sure of it. It had shifted from the landing to somewhere deeper inside the house, behind one of the doors that she had not opened.

She lowered the spoon. She put the tin on the counter. She stood very still and listened to number nine breathe.

The house was quiet for a long time. So quiet that Megan began to think she had imagined it — that the sound had been the house after all, just a pipe or a board or something ordinary that she had misheard because she had been alone too long and her ears had started to invent things.

Then the front door handle turned.

Not turned and opened. Just turned. A slow, deliberate rotation of the brass against the striker plate. Someone — or something — on the other side of the door, testing the lock. Checking whether it held.

Megan was at the far end of the hall. The kitchen was at the back of the house. The front door was at the front. There were four rooms between her and it: the living room, the dining room, the space under the stairs, and the hall itself. She could have run to the door and looked through the letter flap — the flap was missing, she had noticed that on day one, the hole gaping like a mouth in the paint. She could have run and looked out and seen who was standing on the pavement.

She did not move.

The handle stopped turning. It stayed still for a moment. Then it turned back, slowly, to where it had started. As though whatever had been holding it had let go and stepped back.

Megan walked to the living room window and looked out.

The pavement was empty. The street was empty. The lamps were on but they did not reach far — the pools of yellow on the tarmac were small and weak and the gaps between them were vast and dark. She could see number eight, number ten, the gap where number nine’s garden should have been visible but was not, because the hedge was too high and the house was too close to the road.

She could not see the front door from where she was standing.

She could not see who — or what — had been at it.

That night, Megan did not sleep. She sat on the floor of the upstairs bedroom — the one with the curtain that moved — and she listened. The breathing came back at around two in the morning. It was louder this time. Closer. It was coming from the room at the end of the landing, the one with the door that would not open. She had tried the handle on her second night and it had not turned. She had put her shoulder to it and leaned her weight and it had not budged. The key she had found in the shed — the one she had placed on the mantelpiece — did not fit it either. She had tried that too.

The breathing was behind that door.

She sat with her back against the wall and her knees pulled up and her arms wrapped around them and she listened to whatever was in there breathe. It was not the rhythm of a sleeping person. It was not the rhythm of anything she could name. It was too slow. Too deep. It sounded like lungs that had been holding air for a very long time and had forgotten how to let it go.

At some point in the small hours, the breathing stopped.

Megan waited for it to start again. It did not.

Instead, she heard footsteps. Very light. Coming down the stairs. Moving along the landing. Stopping outside the bedroom door.

She did not breathe. She held herself so still that her muscles began to tremble and she had to bite the inside of her cheek to keep them quiet. The footsteps stayed outside the door. She could hear something shifting — fabric against wood, perhaps, or a hand resting lightly on the frame. Then the footsteps moved away. Down the stairs. Into the hall. And then silence again, heavier than before.

Megan did not leave the bedroom. She sat against the wall until the sky began to lighten, until the grey came through the curtain and the house took on the shape of ordinary things again. Then she stood up. Her legs were stiff. Her back was sore. She walked downstairs and made tea with water that was not quite hot enough and drank it standing at the kitchen sink.

She thought about leaving. She had thought about it before — every day, in fact, since the first night, when the house had been new and strange and she had still believed that empty meant safe. Now she knew it did not. She could pack her bag and walk out the back door and keep walking until she reached the town centre and found a hostel or a bench or somewhere else that was not this house. She could leave the key on the mantelpiece. She could leave the dented brass thing that Callum had lost, or left, or placed there on purpose — she still did not know which — and she could leave the thing in the locked room to breathe and shift and wait.

But she did not leave.

Because leaving meant going back to a world where men like Daniel existed. It meant going back to pavements that did not know her name and shelters that smelled of other people’s fear and nights that were colder than this one. Number nine was not safe — she had stopped believing that three days ago — but it was here. It was a place. It had walls and a roof and a pantry with tins in it and a shed with blankets and a chair that sat in a hole in the wall like it had been placed there on purpose.

And Callum was across the road.

She had seen him the night before. Standing at his window. Watching the house. He was thinner than she had expected. Pale. His hair was the colour of something that had not seen sun in a while. He looked like her — not in the face, not in the features, but in the posture. The way people stand when they have been waiting for something to happen and are not sure whether to welcome it or run from it.

She had watched him for a while. He had watched the house. Neither of them had looked at each other.

Megan finished her tea. She put the mug in the sink. She went to the mantelpiece and picked up the dented key. She turned it over in her fingers. The brass was warm. Not from her hand — from something else. She had not held it long enough for it to warm from her hand.

She slipped it into her pocket.

She walked to the front door. She opened it.

The street was empty. The lamps were on. The air smelled of damp and something else — something distant and sweet and burnt. A bonfire, perhaps. Or something else.

Megan stepped out onto the pavement. She stood at the gate and looked across the road at number eleven, at the window where Callum stood at night. The curtain was closed. She could not see inside.

She waited.

She did not know what she was waiting for. But she knew it was coming. The breathing in the locked room. The footsteps on the landing. The feeling that the house was not simply watching her but testing her — seeing how long she would stay, how much she could endure, whether she was the kind of person who could be kept or the kind who would run.

She was the kind who ran. She had proved that in Colchester. She had proved it every time she had left a job or a flat or a town without saying goodbye.

But this time, running meant leaving Callum. And Callum was watching the same house. Callum had a key that did not fit any door in number nine but had been hidden in the shed anyway, as though someone had wanted it to be found. Callum had looked at her the night before with the kind of expression people have when they have seen something they cannot explain and are desperate for someone else to say they are not mad.

Megan stood at the gate. The key was in her pocket, warm against her thigh.

She looked at the house behind her. At the upper window. At the curtain that moved when there was no wind.

The curtain moved now. Just once. A flicker. A shift.

Megan did not move. She did not speak. She simply stood and let the house know she was not afraid of it — or if she was, she was not going to show it. Not yet.

Inside, the breathing stopped again.

And this time, when it started again, it was coming from somewhere else. Not the locked room. Not upstairs.

It was coming from the garden.

From the shed.

From the hole in the wall where the chair sat and the blankets were folded and the key had been hidden.

Megan turned slowly. She looked at the back of the house. At the garden gate. At the darkness beyond it where the shed stood in the damp and the cold and the smell of grass that never quite dried out.

She heard it then. Clear. Definitive. Impossible to mistake.

A breath. Through the garden gate. Through the hedge. Across the pavement.

Someone — or something — was standing at the back of the house. Breathing.

And it was not her.
