Barry and Claire

Chapter One: The Third Meeting

I met Claire three times. The first time was in a supermarket queue. The second time was on a train. The third time was for her birthday, and it was the first time I understood that waiting might be its own kind of love.

She is twenty-three. I am thirty-eight. She has a laugh that starts low and climbs, the way a song builds before the chorus. She wears her hair in a braid that comes loose by afternoon. She bites her lip when she is thinking. She orders coffee black and drinks it slowly, like she is trying to make it last.

I have thought about her between meetings. I have replayed the queue conversation — her complaining about the man in front buying scratch cards, me joking that the till was judging us both. I have replayed the train — her reading a paperback with a broken spine, me pretending to look out the window so I could watch her turn the pages. I have thought about the space between us: fifteen years, a few stations, the quiet hum of the carriage, the way she said my name like she was testing it for size.

For her birthday, I bought a card and a cupcake.

The card was from the shop by the station. It was a birthday card. I do not know if she likes cards. But I wanted her to know that I was celebrating her.

The cupcake was from the bakery on the high street. Vanilla, with pink icing and a sugar flower on top. I carried it carefully. I held it in a paper bag that I had turned sideways so the wind would not catch the icing. I walked fast. I was nervous. I have not been nervous like this since I was twenty-two and buying flowers for a woman who was already seeing someone else.

She lives in a flat above a launderette. The stairs smell of washing powder. Her door is blue. She opens it wearing a dressing gown and a face that says she has been crying, or is about to, or both.

“Barry,” she says. Like she is surprised I came, like she is relieved.

“Happy birthday,” I say. I hold out the cupcake. I hold out the card.

She takes them. She laughs, that climbing laugh, and it is the best sound I have heard all week.

“You didn’t have to,” she says.

“I know.”

She steps aside. She lets me in. The flat is small. There is a sofa with a blanket draped over it. There are books on every surface. There is a candle burning on the windowsill, vanilla, the same as the cupcake. The television is on low — a documentary about seals, I think, though I cannot be sure.

She puts the cupcake on a saucer. She opens the card. She reads it. I wrote something simple. I wrote: *I am glad the queue was long enough for us to meet.*

“Do you want tea?” she asks.

“Please.”

She goes to the kitchen. I hear the kettle fill. I hear the cupboard open. I hear the teaspoon clink against the mug. I stand in the living room and I breathe in. The flat smells of books and candle and something else — lavender, maybe, or soap, or the particular smell of a person who has been tidying their life while it rains.

She comes back with two mugs. She hands me one. She sits on the sofa. I sit beside her. Not too close. Close enough that I can feel the warmth of her arm through the blanket. We drink tea. We watch the seals. I do not know if she is watching. I am not sure I am either.

“I had a bad morning,” she says.

I wait. I do not push. The seals dive into the water. The narrator speaks in a voice that is too calm for the things he is describing.

“Work,” she says. “My boss said something in front of everyone. It wasn’t important. But I cried in the bathroom afterwards. I sat on the floor and I cried for twenty minutes and then I washed my face and came back and nobody noticed.”

I want to say something. I want to say: *I would have noticed.* I want to say: *You are allowed to cry in bathrooms.* I want to say: *I have done it too.* Instead, I say: “Bathrooms are honest places.”

She laughs. It is smaller this time. “That’s a nice way of putting it.”

She finishes her tea. She puts the mug down. She looks at me. She looks at the cupcake.

“Do you want to see the rest of the flat?” she asks.

I nod. She stands. She takes my hand. Her hand is small and warm and slightly sticky from the icing. She leads me through the hallway. There is a bedroom with a duvet that is the colour of wheat. There is a bathroom with a towel rail that is loose. And then there is the bathroom at the end of the hall, the one with the bath.

“I ran it while you were coming over,” she says. “I thought… maybe you’d like to soak. You walked a long way.”

I have. My feet hurt. My shoulders hurt. The weight of the cupcake, the card, the walk, the waiting — it has settled in my bones. I am tired in the way you are tired after a day of holding something precious and not quite knowing where to put it down.

“Okay,” I say.

She closes the door behind us. The flat goes quiet. The seal documentary is a murmur through the wall. The candle flickers in the other room. The bathroom light is warm. The bath is full. Steam rises off the surface like a second breath.

I take my clothes off. I fold them on the closed lid of the toilet. She leaves the room for a moment. I hear her in the hallway. I hear the front door close. I hear the key turn. She is giving me space. She is telling me: *this is yours for as long as you need it.*

I get in. The water is too hot at first, then perfect. I sink down until it touches my chin. I lean my head back. I close my eyes. I listen.

The house settles. The pipes tick. The rain has started again, soft against the window. Somewhere, a car passes. Closer, the towel on the rail moves, just once, as though someone has brushed it. I know she is still in the flat. I know she is sitting in the living room, reading, or watching the television, or staring at the cupcake and the card and wondering if she is allowed to want something this soft this early.

I want to tell her: *you are allowed.*

But words are hard in water. Words are hard in baths. The truth is easier when it is just between you and the steam.

I stay in for an hour. Maybe longer. Time moves differently in hot water. It slows. It softens. It lets you think thoughts you have been avoiding since the supermarket queue, since the train, since the moment you bought the card.

I think about her crying in the bathroom at work. I think about the way she held the cupcake like it was something breakable. I think about the space between our ages and how small it feels when someone laughs at your jokes and means it. I think about the loose towel rail and the candle and the way she led me by the hand through a hallway that I could not see but could feel, the way you feel a tide turning.

I think: *I could love this woman.*

I think: *I might already be doing it.*

When the water goes cold, I get out. I dry myself with a towel that is not mine. It smells of her — lavender, maybe, or soap, or the particular smell of a person who has been trying to be brave. I put her towel back on the rail. I fold my clothes. I open the door.

The flat is dark. The candle has burned down. The documentary is over. She is on the sofa, wrapped in the blanket, asleep. The cupcake is on the saucer. Half of it is gone. The card is on the floor, where it has fallen from the arm of the sofa.

I pick up the card. I put it on the table. I stand for a moment and look at her. She looks younger when she sleeps. The braid has come undone. Her mouth is open a little. She has a crease in her forehead that is not there when she is awake, like her dreams are more serious than her life.

I do not wake her. I do not have to. I write a note on the back of the card — *thank you for the bath* — and I leave it on the table beside the cupcake.

I let myself out. I close the door softly. The stairs smell of washing powder. The street is wet. The air is cool. I walk to the station with my cane and my empty hands and a chest that feels too full for its size.

I have met her three times. I have bought her a cupcake. I have soaked in her bath while she slept on the sofa. I have left a note that says more than I meant it to.

Tomorrow is Tuesday. We walk in the park. I will listen for her rhythm. I will wait for her to say hello.

And when she does, I think I will say it back.

I think I will say it first.
