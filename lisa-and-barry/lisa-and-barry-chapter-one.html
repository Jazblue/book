Lisa and Barry

Chapter One: The Cupcake Reward

Seven weeks of shopping, bathing, and cooking had led to this: a birthday card, a vanilla cupcake, and the faint sound of a couple upstairs.

She is twenty. He is forty. She has hair the colour of autumn leaves and a laugh that fills the hallway. She bites her lip when she is concentrating and hums when she is cooking. She wears trainers that squeak on his hallway floor and brings the smell of the outside world with her — rain, traffic, coffee.

He is blind. He cannot visualise. But he knows when a room is too small because the air feels tight. He knows the shape of an apple by the weight and the smoothness. He knows the distance from his sofa to the kitchen counter by counting steps. He knows Lisa by her voice, her footsteps, and the particular way she says "Barry" as though she is still testing whether he prefers it to "Mr Walsh."

She has been his volunteer carer for seven weeks. She comes three times a week. She buys his food. She assists in the bath — careful, respectful, never lingering longer than needed. She cooks the odd meal: pasta, stir-fry, soup. She sits with him while he eats. She makes sure he takes his tablets. She checks the smoke alarms. She waters the plant on the windowsill even though it is plastic.

Today is her birthday.

He heard her mention it last week, in passing, while she was stacking tins in his cupboard. He wrote it down. He asked the shopkeeper at the corner. He asked the woman at the bakery. He walked to the card shop on the high street and chose a card with a cat on the front because she once said she liked cats, though he has never seen one. He bought a cupcake — vanilla, pink icing, a sugar flower. He carried it home like it was something holy.

She arrives at eleven. She is wearing a coat that is too thin for the weather and a scarf that is too bright. She knocks twice.

"It's me," she says through the door.

"I know," he says. "Your footsteps have a particular bounce today."

She laughs. She lets herself in.

He hears her hang her coat. He hears her squeak across the hall. He hears her stop.

"Is that..." she starts.

"Happy birthday," he says. He holds out the cupcake. He holds out the card.

She makes a sound — not quite a laugh, not quite a cry. She takes them. She opens the card. She reads it in silence. He has written: *Thank you for the last seven weeks. You made the flat feel like a place instead of a space.*

"Barry," she says. Her voice is lower. "You didn't have to."

"I know."

She sits on the sofa. She puts the cupcake on the saucer. She opens the card again. She laughs, that filling laugh, and the flat seems to get bigger when she does.

He sits beside her. Not too close. Close enough to feel the warmth of her arm through the fabric of her jumper. They drink tea. They eat the cupcake. She tells him about her boyfriend — Mark, who works in a garage, who does not understand why she spends so much time at Barry's flat, who says she is "too involved." She does not say it with guilt. She says it the way you say something that is true but not yet solved.

"He doesn't get it," she says.

"What is there to get?" Barry asks.

She is quiet for a moment. "I think he thinks you're a project. Or... I don't know. He says I'm choosing this over us."

Barry says nothing. He listens to the rain on the window. He listens to the traffic outside. He listens to the silence between them and wonders if it is the kind of silence that wants to be filled.

Around four, she makes pasta. She hums while she cooks. She puts extra garlic in because she knows he likes it. They eat at the small table by the window. She tells him about her mother, who lives in Brighton, who calls every Sunday. She tells him about her studies — social work, second year, she is tired but determined. She tells him that she is considering not going home to Mark tonight.

She says it so casually that Barry almost misses it. "I could stay," she says. "If you don't mind the sofa."

He does not mind. He says: "The sofa is yours whenever you want it."

She stays.

Later, after the dishes are done, after the tea is drunk, after the flat is dark and the only sound is the rain and the occasional car, she lies on the sofa with a blanket over her. Barry sits in his armchair by the window. He listens to her breathing slow down. He listens to the building settle.

Then he hears it: from the flat above, the sound of a couple making love. The creak of a bed. The rhythm of movement. The muffled sounds that are more felt than heard. It is not loud. It is intimate. It is the kind of noise that slips through ceiling and floor and wall and finds you whether you want it to or not.

Lisa shifts on the sofa. She makes a small noise — not embarrassment, exactly. Something else.

"Ignore it," Barry says quietly. "It happens."

"I know," she says. "It's just... weird, hearing it when you're staying over."

He smiles. He cannot see her, but he knows she is smiling too. "You get used to it. The walls are thin. The world is noisy."

She is quiet for a long time. Then she says, "Do you ever... you know..."

"What?"

"Pleasure yourself."

Barry laughs. It is a short, surprised sound. "Too much information, Barry," he says, repeating her future words back to her before she says them.

She laughs too. It is a real laugh, loud and warm and completely undoing the tension in the room. "You're impossible," she says.

"I'm honest," he says.

"Same thing, sometimes."

They lie in the dark for a while longer. The sounds from upstairs fade. The rain softens. Lisa's breathing evens out. Barry sits in his chair and thinks about the cupcake, the card, the way she said "Barry" when she came in. He thinks about Mark and the garage and the way Lisa's voice changes when she talks about him — not sad, exactly. Distant. Like she is already somewhere else.

He thinks: *She is staying tonight.*

He thinks: *I should offer her the duvet from the spare room.*

But he does not say it. He lets her stay on the sofa. He lets her be close without being close. He listens to the building and the rain and the sound of her sleeping, and for the first time since his wife left, the flat does not feel empty.

In the morning, she makes toast. She burns it slightly. She laughs about it. She kisses his cheek before she leaves — quick, light, almost accidental — and says, "See you Wednesday."

He says, "Wednesday."

He does not say: *I will listen for your bounce.*

But he will.
