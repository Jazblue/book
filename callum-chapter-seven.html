Chapter Seven: The Door That Would Not Stay Shut

Deborah began checking on Callum in the evenings, after he had finished his homework and Owen was watching cartoons in the living room. She would come upstairs without knocking, open his door, and stand in the frame with a basket of laundry or a complaint about the thermostat.

“You’re in here too long,” she said on Wednesday. “You need fresh air.”

Callum was sitting on the floor with his back against the bed, scrolling through nothing on his phone. He did not need fresh air. He needed the door closed.

“I’m fine,” he said.

Deborah came in anyway. She set the laundry basket on his desk and began sorting socks. She did not seem to notice that he had his headphones on, or that he had turned his phone screen down. She talked about the neighbour’s cat, about the boiler making a noise, about Owen leaving his shoes in the hall. She stayed for ten minutes. Maybe fifteen.

When she left, she pulled the door almost shut, then opened it again. “I can hear the shower running,” she said. “Don’t leave it on too long. The bill is high enough.”

Callum had not had a shower.

On Friday, he was in the bathroom with the door locked. Deborah knocked once, firmly, then turned the handle. The lock held, but only because it was a new lock. He had installed it himself the previous month, when the old one had broken, screwing the new catch into the frame with the screwdriver he had borrowed from the kitchen drawer. Deborah had not commented on it at the time. She had simply watched him do it, arms folded, and said, “You’re getting better at that sort of thing.”

When she knocked, Callum froze. The water was loud in his ears. He turned it off.

“Callum?”

He said nothing.

“I know you’re in there. The light’s on under the door.”

He waited. After a moment, he heard her footsteps go back down the stairs. He did not turn the water back on. He dressed quickly and left the bathroom. Deborah was in the kitchen, pouring milk into a glass. She looked up at him as he came in.

“You didn’t take long,” she said.

“I wasn’t in there long.”

“No. You weren’t.”

That night, Callum lay in bed listening to Owen breathe through the wall. He thought about number nine, about the back door he had opened with the screwdriver, about the house that had breathed the same air for decades and never let it out. He thought about the lock on his bathroom door. It was a good lock. It had held. But the fact that she had tried it — that she had expected it not to be locked — was worse than if she had broken it down.

He got up quietly, opened his wardrobe, and took out the screwdriver. He put it under his pillow.

Outside, the wind was in the trees. Somewhere a dog barked. Number nine sat across the road in the dark, and Callum wondered whether it had a lock on its back door, and if so, whether anyone had ever tried to turn the handle without permission.

He fell asleep thinking about doors that would not stay shut, and the people who kept opening them.
