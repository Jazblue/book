Lisa and Barry

Chapter Three: A Bag by the Door

A profile appears without him asking. A cleaner stays late. And the question of what belongs in his flat becomes harder to answer than he expected.

On Thursday evening, Lisa sent three texts. Barry's phone had an app that could read them aloud — a new thing, AI had improved vastly in the last year, the voice smooth and patient, like someone reading in the next room. He heard the ping. He heard the app begin.

"Hey. I made you a profile."

"It's on the site I told you about. I described you honestly. Nice. Funny. Loves garlic. Hates coffee. Looking for something real. You'll get a match within a week, I promise."

"Don't worry, I didn't use your real name. Let me know when you've listened. x"

He sat on the sofa and listened to them twice. He did not reply. He did not know what to reply. The idea of a profile — of a page, a description, a face he could not see being judged by strangers — made his chest tight. But the voice had been confident. Lisa was always confident. She had been confident about the trunks. She had been confident about the pool. She had been confident that he would swim, that he would laugh, that he would survive the changing room without folding.

She had been right about all of it.

In the morning, on Friday, she arrived before the clock chimed eleven. She was carrying a bucket and a bag of cleaning things. She had mentioned last week that Friday was cleaning day. He had not objected. The flat needed it. The surfaces were dusty. The bathroom had a smell that was not entirely the pipes. The plant on the windowsill was plastic but it still collected dust.

"I'll make short work of it," she said, hanging her coat. "You sit. You listen to the radio. You don't move anything."

He sat in the armchair. He heard her move about the flat. She hummed while she cleaned — a song he did not know, something up-tempo, something that made the flat feel like a kitchen in a film. She wiped the shelves. She emptied the bins. She polished the surfaces. She moved with a purpose that was not the careful, quiet purpose she had when she was bathing him or helping him dress. This was different. This was ownership. This was someone who knew where things went and put them back.

He thought about the Polish lady who had come before Lisa. She had been perfect, Lisa said. She had been with the blind membership charity for three years. She had quit last month. There had been a language barrier — she spoke good English but it was not her first, and sometimes words got stuck, or came out wrong, or simply did not arrive. But she had been gentle. She had been patient. She had known how to wash his hair without getting water in his ears. She had known how to fold his clothes so the seams were on the outside. She had known how to be quiet without being absent.

Lisa did not have the language barrier. Lisa spoke too much, if anything. Lisa talked while she cooked, while she cleaned, while she walked. But she had something the Polish lady did not have — a lightness, a willingness to be messy, a refusal to treat him like he might break. She had touched his arm in the pool without asking. She had laughed at his joke about the trunks. She had told him about Mark without sadness, like she was already deciding.

The flat grew cleaner. The smells changed. The dust disappeared. The bathroom stopped smelling of damp. The kitchen surfaces shone. The clock on the wall ticked. The intercom did not ring. The building was quiet.

Around one, she paused. He heard her set down the bucket. He heard her move to the sofa. She sat beside him, not too close. The cushion dipped.

"Can I ask you something?" she said.

"Anything."

"Can I leave a few things here?"

He turned his head. He could not see her, but he could feel her presence — the warmth of her arm through her t-shirt, the faint smell of cleaning product and lavender. "What sort of things?"

"A nightie. Knickers. You know. Tampons. A change of clothes. Just in case."

"In case of what?"

"In case I stay late. In case the bus doesn't come. In case..." She paused. "In case I want to."

He did not answer immediately. He thought about the sofa. He thought about the spare room, which was full of boxes and old clothes and the duvet his mother had given him before she died. He thought about the Polish lady and the way she had always left before six, the way she had never stayed, the way she had never made the flat feel like it was hers as well as his.

"Where would you put them?" he asked.

"In the spare room. Or the bathroom cupboard. Or under the stairs. Wherever you want."

"The spare room is full."

"Then the bathroom."

"The bathroom cupboard is full of my tablets and my creams."

"Then under the stairs. Or the wardrobe in the hallway. Barry, it's just a bag. It's not a declaration. It's just... in case."

He thought about the bag. He thought about her nightie — soft, probably, something with thin straps and a print he could not picture. He thought about her knickers, her tampons, the smell of her life packed into a small space and left on his floor. He thought about the clock on the wall and the way it chimed every quarter-hour and how, if she left things here, he would know she was coming back.

"Yes," he said.

"Really?"

"Really."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she stood. He heard her go to the hallway. He heard the front door open. He heard her footsteps outside — light, quick, the bounce he knew. She returned with a bag. He heard her set it down in the hallway. He heard her come back to the living room.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't mention it."

"I won't."

She sat down again. She did not move away. The cushion dipped. The flat was clean. The bag was in the hallway. The clock ticked. The world outside was September.

They talked for a while. She told him about the profile. She told him that the app had asked her questions — interests, hobbies, what he was looking for. She had answered honestly. She had said he liked walking, cooking, listening to the radio. She had said he was a good listener. She had said he was not looking for anything casual.

"I didn't say you were a virgin," she said. "That felt like... I don't know. Private."

"Thank you."

"You're welcome."

"And the match?"

"Within a week. I'm sure of it."

He wanted to ask her: *What about you?* He wanted to ask: *What are you looking for?* He wanted to ask: *Are you looking?* But he did not ask. The silence stretched. The clock chimed two. The cleaning was done. The bag was in the hallway. She was sitting beside him on the sofa, close enough that he could feel the warmth of her leg through the fabric.

Then the intercom rang.

It was a different ring — urgent, sharp. Two quick bursts, no pause. Barry shifted in the armchair. He felt his way to the hallway, his hand trailing along the wall. He pressed the button. He did not speak.

"Barry?" It was Lisa's voice, but different. Higher. Faster.

"What is it?"

"I left my keys inside. Can you let me in?"

He unlocked the door. He heard her footsteps. She came in, breathless, flustered. She was carrying a takeaway bag — Chinese, he could smell it, sweet and sour and garlic.

"I'm so sorry," she said. "I ran all the way from the bus stop."

"It's fine."

"I didn't eat lunch. I was going to get something on the way home. But I forgot my keys and I had to come back and..." She trailed off. She set the bag on the kitchen counter. "I can heat it up. If you want some."

"I had toast."

"Barry."

"I had toast," he said. "But I'll have some of yours. If you're offering."

She laughed. It was short, relieved. She opened the bag. She pulled out containers. She heated them in the microwave. The flat filled with the smell of soy sauce and chilli and fried rice. She sat with him at the small table by the window. They ate from the containers with cheap plastic forks.

"I forgot my keys," she said again, like she could not believe it.

"It happens."

"I always forget something. My phone. My wallet. My keys. Last week I left my coat on the bus."

"You came back for it," he said.

"Yeah. Because you were waiting."

The words hung in the air. The clock chimed three. The microwave beeped. The flat was warm with the smell of food and the heat of the day. She reached across the table. She took his hand. She held it. Her fingers were sticky with sauce.

"I should go," she said, after a while.

"You just got back."

"I know. But... I should."

"Friday," he said. It was not a question.

"Friday."

She stood. She collected the containers. She put them in the bin. She washed her hands. She kissed his cheek — quick, light, the same as always. Then she was at the door. She paused. He heard her breathe.

"The bag," she said. "Thank you."

"Don't mention it."

She left. The door closed. The lock clicked. Barry stood in the hallway and listened to the clock. It was three fifteen. The quarters were coming closer together now, the way they do when you are waiting for something. He thought about the bag in the hallway. He thought about the nightie and the knickers and the tampons. He thought about the profile on the screen, the app reading the texts, the match that would come within a week. He thought about the Polish lady and her perfect hands and her language that was almost right. He thought about the clock on the wall, the pendulum swinging, the chime that would ring at three thirty, then three forty-five, then four.

He did not open the bag. He left it in the hallway, where she had put it. He did not touch it. He did not need to. He knew what was inside. He knew what it meant. It meant she was coming back. It meant the flat was not just his anymore. It meant that the line between carer and something else was getting thinner, and he was not sure he wanted to cross it, and he was not sure he could stop himself.

The clock chimed three thirty.

Barry sat in the armchair. He listened to the building. He listened to the street. He listened to the silence of the bag in the hallway, and the silence felt like a promise.

Somewhere above him, the couple were quiet. The walls were still. The world was still.

Barry closed his eyes. He listened to the clock. He counted the minutes until Friday.
