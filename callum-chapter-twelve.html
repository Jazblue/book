Deborah had been watching the house for a week before she admitted it to herself.

Not constantly. Not the way Callum did. Callum was a creature of habit — his window, his time, his angle. Deborah watched in passing, the way people watch things that bother them without knowing why. From the kitchen window while the kettle boiled. From the landing on her way to the bathroom at night. From the passenger seat of the car when she was pulling out of the drive and her eyes drifted automatically toward number nine as they always did.

Something was happening over there. She could feel it the way you feel weather changing — a drop in pressure, a tightening behind the eyes.

On Tuesday morning she saw the girl for the first time.

Deborah was standing at the kitchen table, spreading jam on toast for Owen, when movement across the road caught her. She looked up. Number nine’s front door had opened. A girl came out. She was young — Deborah’s age, or close enough that Deborah felt the difference in her bones and resented it — wearing a t-shirt that barely reached her thighs and nothing else, barefoot on the cold pavement. Her hair was dark and unwashed, pulled back with something that looked like a hairband that had seen better days.

She walked down the path to the gate. She was carrying something — a paperback, maybe, or a notebook. She set it on the wall by the postbox and bent to retrieve it. Her back was to Deborah. The morning sun came through the trees and the light was grey and thin but Deborah could see the shape of her through the cotton of the shirt and the way her shoulders moved when she stretched.

She looked like somebody who had nowhere else to go.

Deborah’s first thought was not kind. It was sharp and fast and entirely without mercy: that girl.

Her second thought was: what is she doing in number nine?

The house had been empty for months. The previous owners — a couple, older, the kind who kept their curtains drawn and their garden tidy and never said hello — had moved out in the spring. Deborah had not seen anyone going in or out since. The house had taken on the quality of things that are not simply vacant but have been left deliberately, as though the rooms inside held something the owners could not carry with them.

And now there was this girl.

Deborah watched her collect the paperback from the wall and walk back up the path. The girl paused at the door. She looked up — not across the road, not toward Deborah’s window, but at the upper floor of her own house. Her face changed. Just for a second. Something passed across it — fear, Deborah thought, or the memory of fear. Then she went inside and the door shut behind her.

Deborah stood at the kitchen table with the knife in her hand and the toast on the plate and Owen talking about something in the garden she was only half listening to.

The girl had not looked at her. That was the worst part, in a way. If the girl had looked — if she had met Deborah’s eyes across the road and acknowledged her — Deborah could have decided what kind of person she was dealing with. A squatter. A runaway. Someone’s daughter who had made bad choices. She could have decided, and then she could have acted.

But the girl hadn’t looked. She had looked at her own house like it was the thing that was watching her.

That night, Deborah lay awake and listened to Owen breathe and listened to the house settle and thought about number nine.

She had not told Callum about the girl. She had thought about it. The words had been sitting in her throat all day — there’s someone living in the house across the road — but she had not said them. Partly because she did not know what else to add. Partly because she had learned, over the years, that Callum’s silences were not the same as other people’s silences. When he went quiet, something was happening inside him that he was not ready to share. If she told him about the girl, he would go over there. She was sure of it.

And she was not sure she wanted him to.

The next morning, she saw the girl again. This time she was wearing jeans and a hoodie, standing at the gate, waiting. The postman came and handed her something — a parcel — and she took it with both hands like she was receiving a gift. The postman said something to her. She nodded. She did not smile.

Deborah watched from behind the kitchen curtain.

When the postman had gone, the girl stood on the pavement for a moment holding the parcel. Then she looked up at Deborah’s window. Directly at it. As though she had known Deborah was standing there all along.

Deborah stepped back from the curtain. Her heart was doing something it had not done in a long time — not racing, exactly, but moving differently, as though it had forgotten its rhythm and was trying to find it again.

She went downstairs and made Owen’s packed lunch and told him to put his shoes on and did not look out of the window again.

But she thought about the girl. And she thought about Callum. And she thought about the screwdriver she had taken from him and the lock on the bathroom door he had installed and the way he had stood at the kitchen table saying I need it with a look on his face she had not been able to read.

She needed to speak to him. She needed to sit him down and say: there is something happening in that house and you are not part of it.

The problem was, she had been saying versions of that sentence for years. And Callum had been ignoring them for just as long.

That evening, she broached it. Gently. Over lasagne, which was Owen’s favourite and therefore the only meal Deborah could rely on to keep him talking for longer than three minutes.

“That house across the road,” she said. “Number nine. Have you noticed anything? People going in and out?”

Callum was cutting his lasagne into small pieces. He did not look up. “No.”

“There’s a girl living there. I’ve seen her. She came to the door this morning in a t-shirt. Collecting something from the postman.”

Callum kept cutting. “Maybe she’s visiting.”

“She’s not visiting. She lives there. The door opened like she owned it.” Deborah paused. Her voice was level but her hands were not. She put her fork down. “Callum. I need you to stay away from that house.”

He looked up then. His face was careful. The face he used when he was thinking about whether to lie. “Why?”

“Because it’s not safe. Because it’s been empty and now it’s not and I don’t know who she is. Because you’ve been acting strangely for weeks and I don’t know what to think anymore.”

“I haven’t been acting strangely.”

“You stand at the window all night. You sneak out. You left a screwdriver on the table that wasn’t mine. You—”

She stopped. She had almost said you left a key under your pillow. She had not known about the key until Owen had mentioned it, two days before, in the casual way children mention things that should not be mentioned: Callum has something under his pillow. She had asked Callum about it. He had said it was nothing. She had believed him because believing was easier than not believing.

“I’m fine,” Callum said.

“That girl,” Deborah said. “If she’s in trouble, it’s not our problem. If she’s squatting, it’s the council’s problem. You are not to go over there.”

Callum said nothing.

Deborah stood and cleared the plates. She washed them at the sink with the hot tap running hard, the way she did when she was angry. Owen sat at the table drawing. The house was quiet except for the water and the scrape of plastic on ceramic and Owen’s pencil moving across paper.

She thought about the girl’s face at the window. The way she had looked at the house like it was threatening her. The way Deborah had felt, standing behind her curtain, like an intruder in her own observation.

She did not know it yet, but she would see the girl again. And next time, the girl would not be alone.

And next time, Callum would be with her.
