Deborah stood on the pavement of Alder Lane at half past nine on a Thursday morning and did not know what she was doing.

She had kissed Owen goodbye at the gate — he was at the age where he preferred the walk to school with his friends over the walk with his mother, and she let him, because independence was important and because she needed the time afterwards to think — and then she had stood on the step and watched him turn the corner and she had not gone back inside.

Instead she had walked down the drive and along the pavement and across the road and up the path to number nine.

She told herself it was practical. The girl — Megan, Callum had let the name slip the night before over lasagne, a mistake he had tried to cover with a cough — was living in a house that was not hers. That was squatters’ territory. That was something the council needed to know about. Deborah was a responsible adult. She was doing her civic duty.

The truth was closer to something uglier. The truth was that she had been watching the girl for days and the girl had not looked at her and Callum had been acting like a stranger in his own home and something in the air over number nine felt like the kind of pressure you get before a storm and Deborah had not slept properly since Tuesday and she wanted, more than anything, for someone to tell her she was not going mad.

She knocked on the door.

Three times. Firm. Not aggressive — she was not that kind of person — but not soft either. The kind of knock that said I am here and I am not leaving until you answer.

She waited.

The house was quiet. Not the quiet of an empty house — she had stood outside it enough times to know the difference. An empty house has a particular quality of silence, like the rooms are holding their breath. This was different. This was the silence of a house with someone inside it who was deciding whether to answer the door.

Deborah knocked again.

Footsteps. Slow. Hesitant. Coming down the hallway. The latch clicked. The door opened a few inches and Megan was standing there, wearing the same t-shirt as on Tuesday and eyes that had not slept.

She looked at Deborah. Deborah looked at her. They stood in the small space between the door and the pavement and did not speak for a long time.

“You’re Callum’s mother,” Megan said.

It was not a question.

“I am. And you’re living in my neighbour’s house without permission.”

Megan did not close the door. She did not open it wider. She held it exactly where it was, as though the distance between them — three feet of air and the threshold of a house that was not hers — was something she could control. “I’m not staying.”

“You’re here now.”

“I know.”

Deborah’s voice was steady. She had practiced what she would say in the car on the way over. You need to leave. This is not your home. You’re making people uncomfortable. But the words came out differently. Smaller. Less certain.

“Can I come in?” she said.

Megan’s eyebrows went up. It was the first expression she had worn that was not carefully guarded. Surprise. Genuine, unplanned surprise. “Why?”

“Because I want to see where you’re living. Because I want to know what’s going on in this house. Because my son has been watching it for weeks and I need to understand why.”

Megan looked at her for a moment longer. Then she opened the door wider. “Don’t touch anything.”

Deborah stepped inside.

The hallway was cold. Not the cold of a house with the heating off — she had been in plenty of cold houses. This was colder. It was a deliberate cold. A cold that had been waiting for her. She felt it on her forearms, on the back of her neck, in the space between her shoulder blades. She told herself it was nothing. She told herself it was damp. She told herself it was early morning and the house had been shut up for months and of course it would be cold.

But she did not believe herself.

Megan led her through to the kitchen. It was clean — cleaner than Deborah had expected. The tins were arranged on the counter by size. A spoon was resting on the draining board. The kettle was there, old and stained, but it looked like it had been used that morning.

“You’re not from around here,” Deborah said.

“Colchester.”

“What brought you to Alder Lane?”

Megan turned. She was leaning against the counter. Her arms were crossed. Her feet were bare on the lino. There was bruising on her jaw — faint now, yellowing at the edges, but there. Deborah saw it and something tightened in her chest.

“I left,” Megan said.

“Left what?”

“A man.”

Deborah waited. She had learned that waiting was more effective than pushing. With Owen, with Callum, with the people who came to the door selling charity tickets or god, waiting was the only tool that ever worked.

Megan told her. Briefly. The flat in Colchester. The way it had started — charming, attentive, the kind of man who brought you flowers and knew your favourite film. The way it had continued — the questions about where you had been, the comments about your friends, the first time he had grabbed her wrist and held it too tight and said I was only joking. The last night — the shouting, the shove, the split lip, the bus station at half past one with a bag and a wallet and nothing else. The bus that had taken her somewhere she remembered from childhood. The house that had looked empty. The back door that had not been locked.

She did not cry when she told it. She did not look away. She told it the way people tell things they have rehearsed in their heads a hundred times and have finally decided to speak out loud.

Deborah listened.

When Megan finished, Deborah said: “You can’t stay here.”

“I know.”

“It’s not safe.”

Megan laughed. It was a short, sharp sound. Not humour. Recognition. “I know that too.”

“Then why are you still here?”

Megan looked at the kitchen table. There was a dented brass key sitting on it. The same key Deborah had seen Callum put under his pillow. The same key Megan must have found in the shed.

“Because of the house,” she said quietly. “It’s not empty. Not really. There’s something in it. Something that was here before I arrived. Something that doesn’t want me to leave.”

Deborah felt the cold in the kitchen deepen. It was not her imagination. It was not the damp. It was something else — something that had been listening to their conversation and had decided to make itself known. She looked at the doorway to the hall. The shadow was there. Not a person. Not quite. But the shape of a person, tall, standing just out of sight, watching.

She looked at Megan. Megan had seen it too. Her eyes had flicked to the doorway and then back to Deborah’s face and she had not said anything. She had not needed to.

“What is it?” Deborah said.

“I don’t know.”

“You hear it?”

“Every night. Breathing. A voice in the walls. Footsteps on the landing when I know I’m alone.”

Deborah thought about Callum. About the screwdriver. About the lock on the bathroom door. About the way he had stood at the kitchen table saying I need it with a look on his face that had frightened her more than his silence ever had.

“My son,” she said. “He’s been watching this house too.”

Megan nodded. “I know. I’ve seen him. He stands at his window at night. Same time every night. He’s been doing it since before I got here.”

“He hasn’t told me anything.”

“He hasn’t told me anything either. But we’ve… communicated.” Megan paused. “He sees it. Whatever it is. He’s been seeing it for longer than I have.”

Deborah’s throat went tight. “What do you mean, he’s been seeing it?”

“I mean he’s been watching this house because there’s something in it that he noticed before anyone else did. Before me. Before you. He was already here. He was already waiting.”

Deborah looked around the kitchen. The cold was everywhere now. It was in the corners of the room. It was in the doorway. It was in the space between her and Megan, thickening, pressing, as though the house had decided that Deborah was a threat and was preparing to defend itself.

She thought about leaving. She thought about taking Megan by the arm and pulling her out of the house and calling the police and having the whole thing sorted before lunch.

But she did not move.

Because Megan was right. Because Callum had been watching this house before the girl arrived. Because something in the walls was patient and deliberate and had been waiting for Deborah to come inside so it could show her what it was capable of.

And because, deep down, Deborah had known about the locked room for weeks. Not the room itself — she had never seen it — but the presence of it. The weight of it. The way number nine felt when you looked at it from the kitchen window, the way it felt like a house that was holding something inside and refusing to let it out.

“I need to see it,” Deborah said.

Megan’s face changed. “What?”

“The room. The locked one.”

“Deborah, you don’t.”

“I need to.”

She said it the way mothers say things when they have made a decision and are not open to argument. The way she had said no more to Callum when she took the screwdriver.

Megan hesitated. Then she sighed. She pushed off the counter and walked to the hallway. Deborah followed.

The stairs creaked. The cold grew stronger as they climbed — not gradually, but in steps, as though each flight brought them closer to whatever was waiting at the top. Deborah’s breath was visible now. Her fingers were numb. She pulled her cardigan tighter around herself and kept going.

The landing was longer than she had expected. The house was deeper than it looked from the road. There were doors on either side — three of them, all closed, all with the same kind of knob, the same kind of lock. Megan stopped at the last one. The door at the end.

She put her hand on the handle. She did not turn it. She simply held it there, her palm against the brass, her face tight.

“I’ve never opened it,” she said.

“Why not?”

“Because I can hear it. Breathing. From inside. Every night. Sometimes during the day. It never stops.”

Deborah looked at the door. It was ordinary. A white door, scuffed at the bottom, the paint slightly yellowed, a number in pencil on the frame — 3, she thought, or possibly 8. It looked like every other door in every other house on Alder Lane.

She reached out. Her hand hovered over the handle.

The cold from behind the door was so intense it made her eyes water.

She took her hand away.

Megan looked at her. Deborah shook her head. Not because she was afraid — not entirely. Because something behind the door was listening, and it knew she was there, and it was waiting for her to make a mistake.

“We need to go,” Deborah said.

Megan did not argue. She led the way downstairs. The cold lessened with each step but did not leave entirely. It clung to the walls. It followed them down the stairs and along the hall and out through the front door and onto the pavement, where the morning sun was thin and grey and the street was empty except for a van parked at number seven and a child’s scooter abandoned on the pavement outside number four.

Deborah stood on the step. She looked at Megan. Megan looked at her.

“You’ll tell him?” Megan said.

“Tell who?”

“Callum. You’ll tell him what’s in the house.”

Deborah thought about her son. About the way he had been lately. About the screwdriver and the lock and the key under his pillow. About the way he looked at number nine when he thought she was not looking — not with curiosity, but with recognition. As though he knew something about the house that he had never said out loud.

“Yes,” she said.

She meant it. She would tell him. She would sit him down at the kitchen table and she would say: I have been to the house. I have heard what is in the walls. You are not to go near it again.

She did not know, yet, that it was already too late for that.

She turned and walked back across the road. Megan stood at the gate and watched her go. When Deborah reached her own front door, she looked back. Megan was still there. Still watching. Still holding the space between the two houses like a thread that had not yet been pulled taut.

Deborah went inside. She locked the back door. She locked the front door. She checked the lock on the bathroom door — the one Callum had installed — and found it secure.

She stood in the hallway and listened to her own house breathe around her.

It sounded, for a moment, very like number nine.
