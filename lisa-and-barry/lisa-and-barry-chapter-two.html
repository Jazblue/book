Lisa and Barry

Chapter Two: The Drawer

The kitchen was too small for two people and their silence. Lisa opened a drawer and found things Barry had forgotten he owned.

Lisa arrived at noon. She was carrying a Tupperware container and a pair of scissors.

"Your mother sent these," she said. "She found them in her loft. She said you would know what they are."

He did not know what they were. He could hear them — a flat tin box wrapped in tissue paper, tied with string. He could hear the scissors opening and closing. He could hear her unwrap the tin. He could hear the clink of something metal against metal.

"It is a compass," she said. "Old-fashioned. The kind you hold in your hand and it points north. Your father gave it to you. He gave it to you the year you were born."

"I had a compass," he said.

"You had a compass. You lost it somewhere. She says you always carried it in your jacket."

"I stopped wearing jackets."

"You still have the compass. You just did not know where it was. That is how it works. You lose things. You find them again. You do not always remember losing them."

She held it out. He took it. The metal was cold. It weighed more than he remembered. He turned it over in his fingers. He felt the glass face, the tiny needle that trembled and pointed. North. The direction he had always faced, even when he could not see it.

"Thank you," he said.

"Do not thank me. Thank your mother. She kept it all this time."

He put it in his jacket — the one he kept by the door, the one he wore on days when he wanted to feel presentable. The jacket hung on the back of the kitchen chair. It was his best jacket. He did not wear it often because it made him feel like someone else.

At half past twelve, Lisa started cutting wrapping paper. It was a Tuesday morning. She had said she wanted to make a birthday present. He did not know it was his birthday until she said so. She had kept it to herself — the day, the month, the year of his birth — because she had found it written in his passport, which was on the shelf above the kitchen table, next to a photograph of his father.

"I am not good at wrapping," she said. "Watch."

She wrapped the compass slowly. The paper was brown, the kind with a pattern of small leaves on it. She folded the corners. She taped them flat. She wrote something on the card that was tied to the string. She did not show him what it said.

He listened to her hands. The scissors. The tape. The rustle of the paper. The particular sounds that a person makes when they are trying to be thoughtful and are not very good at it.

"You are not good at this," he said.

"I am trying."

"You are trying hard."

She laughed. Not the filling laugh. This one was smaller. The one she made when she was alone, or when he made her laugh, or when she was surprised by something kind.

They ate pasta at the small table by the window. She had made it with garlic, because she knew he liked it. The kitchen was too small for two people and their silence. She sat on one side of the table. He sat on the other. The chair scraped against the floor when she shifted. The fork clinked against the plate. The clock on the wall ticked like a heartbeat.

"Do you remember," she said, "when I told you about Mark?"

"You told me he worked in a garage."

"Yes. I told you he worked in a garage. He does not like me spending so much time here. He says I am too involved." She paused. "I told you that because I wanted you to know what it was like. What it feels like to care about someone and not know how to stop."

"You do not have to stop."

"I am not going to stop. I am just going to be honest about it."

"That is honest."

"It is." She pushed her plate aside. She looked at him. He could not see her, but he could hear her breathing change — the intake, the pause, the slower release that meant she was about to say something important.

"I want to tell you something," she said. "And I am not sure you are ready to hear it."

"Try me."

"I called Mark yesterday. I called him twice. He did not answer. I left a message. I said, 'I am coming to the flat.' He called me back. I did not answer again."

"Why?"

"Because I am not sure where I am going. Because I am not sure who I am becoming. Because I met you and now I do not know what is real and what is not."

The silence that followed was not uncomfortable. It was the kind of silence that has weight — the kind you feel when two people are in a small kitchen and the walls are thin and the clock is loud and the pasta has gone cold between them.

"I am a virgin," he said. He did not know why he said it. It just came out, the way things come out when you are sitting across from someone who makes the air feel different.

"Oh," she said.

"I have not been with anyone. I have not been with anyone since I was fifteen. That is a long time. That is most of my adult life. I have just... waited."

She was quiet for a long time. She did not fill the silence. She let it sit between them, heavy with honesty, like the weight of the Tupperware container on the table that she had brought and was now eating from.

"You are not waiting," she said finally. "You are sitting. You are here. That is different."

"Is it?"

"Yes." She reached across the table and took his hand. Her fingers were warm. Her hand was calloused from care work, from the gym, from the small life she lived with her hands. She held his hand and did not let go.

"I want to be with you," she said. "Not because I am a carer. Not because I feel sorry for you. Because I want to. I want to sit in this kitchen and hear you breathe and listen to the clock and feel the silence between us. I want to be the person you tell things to. I want to be the person you wait for."

He squeezed her hand. It was the first time he had initiated touch in return. It was the first time he had reached out without being prompted. It felt brave. It felt stupid. It felt like standing on the edge of a cliff and deciding to jump.

"What if it is not enough?" he asked. "What if I want more than this? What if I want to be held? To be... held. Not sitting. Not touching hands. Something more."

She did not pull away. She held his hand tighter. "Then we talk about it. When you are ready. Not tonight. Not now. But someday. When you are ready."

"And if I am never ready?"

"Then you are never ready. That is okay. I am not going anywhere."

She rested her head on the table. Her hair fell across the surface. He could smell lavender and something else — the particular scent of a person who has been outside in September heat, warm and alive.

"Do you know what I think?" she said.

"What?"

"I think the compass was never about the compass. It was about direction. When you could not see the way, it pointed north. When I came into your kitchen, I was pointing toward you. I did not know which way was north. But I knew which way was you."

"I think you are right," he said.

"I think we are pointing the same way."

She did not say anything else for a long time. The clock continued. The pasta was cold. The compass sat on the table between them, wrapped in brown paper with the leaf pattern.

"I should go," she said eventually.

"Lisa —"

"Friday. Same time. Same kitchen."

"Friday."

She stood. She put her jacket on. She pulled the scissors out of her bag and placed them on the counter. She kissed his cheek. It was quick, light, almost accidental — the kind of kiss that says *I will be back* and *I am not leaving yet*. Then she was gone. The footsteps faded. The kitchen was empty. The compass remained, the way it always had, but it felt different now. It felt like a thing that had been found after being lost for a very long time, and the finding was not about the thing itself but about the person who had looked for it and not given up.

Barry sat alone. He listened to the clock. It chimed twelve. The quarters ticked by. The flat was quiet.

He thought: *I am a virgin.*

He thought: *She knows.*

He thought: *She said she is not going anywhere.*

He did not know what to do with any of it. But he knew this: the compass was not about the compass. It was about direction. And he was no longer facing the wrong way.

He thought: *Friday is a long time away.*

He thought: *I will count the quarters until she comes back.*
