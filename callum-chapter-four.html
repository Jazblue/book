Tuesday was the day he almost knocked.

He’d planned it the night before, lying awake while the house settled around him. Simple enough: walk to number nine, knock on the front door, see what happened. Worst case, nobody answered and he walked away looking like an idiot. Best case, someone opened it and the mystery ended in five minutes of awkward conversation.

He left the house at half past nine, told Deborah he was going to the library.

She didn’t ask why. She’d stopped asking why about most things.

The morning was overcast, the kind of sky that felt close, like the air had thickened and forgotten to move. Callum crossed the road with his hands in his pockets, watching number nine get larger as he approached. The front garden looked different in daylight — not menacing, just neglected. Weeds clustered around the gatepost. The path had moss in the cracks. A curtain hung slightly askew in the downstairs window to the left of the door.

He stopped at the gate.

The house was silent. Not the held silence of the night before, just ordinary empty-house silence. The sort of place where the only sound is the fridge and the distant road.

He put his hand on the gate. It was cold. The metal of the latch was rusted.

He pushed. It opened.

The path to the door was longer than he expected. Each step felt deliberate, as if the house was counting them. He kept his eyes on the front door — cream paint, peeling at the bottom, an old letterbox with a brass flap that had gone green.

He reached the door.

Raised his hand.

Knocked.

The sound was dull, absorbed by the wood. He waited.

Nothing.

He knocked again, harder.

Still nothing.

He pressed his ear to the door. The wood was cold through his cheek. He held his breath and listened.

Faint. Very faint. A sound from inside. Not music. Not voices. Something mechanical. A hum, maybe. Or a fan.

He pulled back.

Then he saw the letterbox flap move.

Not opened from the inside. Shifted. Just slightly. As if someone on the other side had pressed their eye to it.

Callum stepped back fast, his heel catching on a loose stone. He righted himself without taking his eyes off the letterbox. The flap stayed where it was. The hum continued, barely audible.

He turned and walked home. Didn’t run — that would have been admitting something — but he walked quickly, the way you do when you’ve just confirmed that the thing you hoped was imaginary is real.

By the time he got back to the house his hands were shaking. He made tea and sat at the kitchen table with it, both hands around the mug, staring at nothing.

Deborah came down an hour later. She looked at him. She didn’t say anything, just went to the kettle and made her own drink.

“I think someone’s in number nine,” he said.

She didn’t act surprised. She sat opposite him and wrapped her hands around her mug. “I know.”

“You know?”

“I’ve seen the lights. At night. Upstairs front.”

“Why didn’t you say anything?”

“Because it’s not our business, Callum. People live their lives. If they want to keep their curtains shut, that’s their choice.”

“This is different.”

“How?”

He couldn’t explain it. Not really. The feeling of being watched. The letterbox moving. The hum that shouldn’t have been there. The way the house made the street feel like it was holding its breath.

“It just is.”

Deborah drank her tea in silence. Outside, rain began again, soft at first, then harder, tapping the kitchen window in a rhythm that felt like patience.

“Don’t go in,” she said.

“I haven’t.”

“If you do, don’t tell me about it.”

That was the closest she ever came to giving permission. Callum knew it. He also knew she was scared — not of number nine specifically, but of the shape his curiosity took when it latched onto something. She’d seen it before. She’d watched it grow.

He went back upstairs after she left for work. Opened his laptop. The probate record sat at the top of his notes. He highlighted it, then wrote a new line underneath.

M. H. Alderton. Probate 2020. No next of kin located. House still occupied.

He stared at the sentence. It didn’t make sense, not in any normal way. A dead man’s house with no relatives should be empty. Should be cleared out, sold, turned over. Instead, number nine sat there like a held breath, lights flickering on at three in the morning, hums behind the door, movements behind cream curtains.

He thought about the back door. The Yale. The rotten frame.

He thought about the screwdriver in the kitchen drawer.

The cursor blinked.

Outside, the rain kept falling. Somewhere down the lane a car door slammed. The house settled around him.

Callum closed the laptop.

He didn’t go in that night. But he lay awake knowing that he would. Soon. The decision had been made the moment he’d knocked on the door and heard the hum on the other side.

The question was no longer whether.

It was what he’d find when he got there.
