Lisa and Barry

Chapter Two: The Crack in the Bench

The oak tree was always the same. The crack in the bench was always changing. Lisa sat beside Barry in a way she had not sat before, and he could hear the distance closing.

On Wednesday morning, Lisa arrived wearing a yellow dress that was too bright for September. She had her hair in a ponytail. She was carrying a black sports bag.

"I'm going to the park," she said, like it was the simplest thing in the world.

He had not forgotten. She had mentioned it last week, while she was chopping onions in his kitchen. She had said the oak tree was coming into leaf. She had said it would be good to sit outside. She had said, "I'll take you," like it was the simplest thing in the world.

It was not the simplest thing. The simplest thing was tea. The simplest thing was toast. The simplest thing was sitting in the armchair by the window and listening to the rain. A walk to the park required shoes. It required being seen. It required the crack in the bench that he knew about but had never been able to describe.

He had not walked to the park since he was a boy. His father had taken him once, to a park that no longer existed. He remembered the gravel paths, the sound of his footsteps, the way his father had held his hand and counted the lampposts like they were something precious. He had not been back since.

"I bought you something," Lisa said. She opened her bag. She pulled out a small paper bag. Inside was a vanilla cupcake, pink icing, a sugar flower on top. "I made one. It's not very good. I burned the edges."

"How much?" he asked.

"Don't worry about it."

"Lisa."

"My money. My choice. You need it. Birthday treat."

He took it. The paper bag was warm. He held it like it was something holy.

At half past ten, the intercom rang. The usual ring: two short bursts, a pause, then a longer buzz. He shifted in his armchair. He felt his way to the hallway, his hand trailing along the wall, counting the doorframes, finding the stairs by the change in air pressure. He lived on the ground floor. The flat was small — one bedroom, one bathroom, a kitchen that opened onto a living room. He liked it small. Small was knowable. Small was safe.

He pressed the button. He unlocked the door. He heard her footsteps on the path, then on the hall tiles. She was wearing trainers. He could hear them squeak. She was wearing a coat that rustled. She was wearing the yellow dress under it. He knew because she had told him, and because he could smell something floral — lavender, maybe, or soap — that was not the coat.

"Are you ready?" she asked.

He had his blind stick by the door. He had dressed carefully that morning — the shirt he wore when he wanted to feel presentable, the trousers he pressed the night before. He looked like himself, not the version of himself who sat in the armchair listening to the clock.

"I'm ready."

She took his arm. She led him out. The walk to the park was fifteen minutes. She talked while they walked. She talked about her mother. She talked about her studies. She talked about a film she had seen the night before. She did not talk about Mark. She had not mentioned Mark for three days. Barry did not ask why.

The park was quiet. September light came through the leaves in soft gold fragments. The air smelled of cut grass and distant rain. Lisa found the oak tree. She pointed. She had told him about it before — the one with the crack in the bench, the one that leaned over the path like it was listening.

"The bench," she said.

"I know."

She sat. He sat. The crack in the bench was familiar to him — he had sat on it a hundred times, felt its shape through his clothes, known every dip and rise. But today it felt different. Today it was not just a surface. It was a place where two people sat close enough that the silence between them had weight.

She took the cupcake from the bag. She broke it in half. She gave him the larger piece. He ate it slowly. The icing was sweet. The sponge was dry in the middle. It was, he thought, perfect.

"Do you remember," she said, "when you told me about the crack in the bench? At the launderette?"

"Which one?"

"The one you said was ours."

He smiled. "I said that?"

"You said, 'I will listen for her rhythm. I will wait for her to say hello.'" She paused. "You said that about me. About sitting on the bench. About waiting."

"I think you might be remembering someone else's words."

"Maybe." She laughed. That filling laugh. The one that filled the flat when she came in.

"So," she said. "You were telling me about your virginity the other day. In the café."

"I was not telling you anything."

"You were telling me I was the first person you told. That is telling me something."

He did not answer. He listened to the birds in the oak tree. He listened to the traffic beyond the park gates. He listened to the silence between them and wondered if it was the kind of silence that wanted to be filled.

"I have not been with anyone," he said. He did not know why he said it. It just came out, the way things come out when you are sitting in a park with someone who makes the air feel different.

"Oh," she said.

"It is not something I am proud of. I know that. I know I should not wait. I know I should not be—" He stopped. He breathed. "I should not be waiting for something that might not come."

She was quiet for a long time. She did not fill the silence. She let it sit between them, heavy with honesty.

"You are not waiting," she said finally. "You are sitting. You are here. That is different."

"Is it?"

"Yes." She turned to face him. He could not see her, but he could hear her breathing change. The intake. The pause. The slower release.

"I have been thinking," she said. "About what you said in the café. About the trunks. About the pool." She paused. "I am not going to make you comfortable, Barry. Not because I think comfort is better than honesty. But because I think you deserve to know that the way you are — the way you exist in a body that the world is not built for — that is not something that repels me. It does not make me feel sorry for you. It makes me feel... I do not have a word for it. It makes me feel like I want to be closer to you. Not because I feel obligated. Because I want to."

The words landed. They were heavy. They were warm. He felt them in his chest, like stones being placed carefully, one by one.

"I am not going anywhere," she said. "I have not been going anywhere. Mark called. He called twice. I did not answer. Not because I am hiding anything. Because I am not there yet. I am not sure where there is, or what there looks like, or whether I have the courage to go there alone."

"You are not alone," he said.

"I know."

She reached over and took his hand. Her fingers were warm. Her hand was calloused from care work, from the gym, from the small life she lived with her hands. She held his hand and did not let go.

"I want to be with you," she said. "Not because I am a carer. Not because I feel sorry for you. Because I want to. I want to sit on this bench and hear you breathe and listen to the clock and feel the silence between us. I want to be the person you tell things to. I want to be the person you wait for."

He squeezed her hand. It was the first time he had initiated touch in return. It was the first time he had reached out without being prompted. It felt brave. It felt stupid. It felt like standing on the edge of a cliff and deciding to jump.

"What if it is not enough?" he asked. "What if I want more than this? What if I want to be held? To be... held. Not sitting. Not touching hands. Something more."

She did not pull away. She held his hand tighter. "Then we talk about it. When you are ready. Not tonight. Not now. But someday. When you are ready."

"And if I am never ready?"

"Then you are never ready. That is okay. I am not going anywhere."

She rested her head on his shoulder. He could feel her hair against his cheek. He could smell lavender and something else — the particular scent of a person who has been outside in September heat, warm and alive.

"Do you know what I think?" she said.

"What?"

"I think the crack in the bench was never about the bench. It was about the space between people. The space that two people leave when they sit close enough to touch but not close enough to be held."

"I think you are right," he said.

"I think we are filling it."

She did not say anything else for a long time. The birds continued. The light continued. The cupcake was finished. The park was quiet.

"I should go," she said eventually.

"Lisa —"

"Friday. Same time. Same bench."

"Friday."

She kissed his cheek. It was quick, light, almost accidental — the kind of kiss that says *I will be back* and *I am not leaving yet*. Then she was gone. The footsteps faded. The park was empty. The crack in the bench remained, the way it always had, but it felt different now. It felt like a place where two people had sat and chosen each other, not out of obligation, not out of loneliness, but out of the quiet, stubborn decision that they would rather be together than apart.

Barry sat alone. He listened to the clock. It chimed twelve. The quarters ticked by. The flat was quiet.

He thought: *I am a virgin.*

He thought: *She knows.*

He thought: *She said she is not going anywhere.*

He did not know what to do with any of it. But he knew this: the crack in the bench was not about the bench. It was about the space between people. And he was no longer sitting alone in it.

He thought: *Friday is a long time away.*

He thought: *I will count the quarters until she comes back.*
