Chapter Five: The Back Door

The kitchen clock said ten minutes to midnight when Callum finally put on his trainers.

Deborah was asleep upstairs. Owen too, though Owen’s sleep was the shallow kind that broke if you looked at him wrong. The house breathed its usual nighttime sounds: fridge hum, pipes ticking as they cooled, distant traffic on the main road.

Callum didn’t want to go outside. He wanted to stay in his room with the monitor glow and the safe distance of screens. But the figure at the window had not left him alone. It sat in his mind the way certain images do, sharp and recurring, demanding to be looked at directly.

He opened the back door quietly. The latch clicked louder than he expected, so he froze on the step, listening. Nothing moved. The night smelled of damp grass and the faint chemical tang someone was using to kill weeds on the neighbour’s drive.

Number nine was opposite.

Callum crossed the road in his socks because trainers on wet tarmac made too much noise. His feet went cold the moment he left the pavement. The grass at the edge of number nine’s front garden was taller than everywhere else, like it had been deliberately left. Brambles clustered around the gatepost, and the path to the front door was cracked in a way that suggested it hadn’t seen a footstep in months.

No lights on anywhere. No sound from inside.

He checked the front of the house first. Cream curtains, shut. The downstairs window to the left of the door was painted shut on the inside — he could see the white paint around the frame. Upstairs, the same blank indifference to the street.

But the back.

Callum moved along the side of the house, keeping close to the wall. The neighbour’s security light flicked on as he passed their fence, then off again when he stepped clear. His heart was doing something stupid and fast in his chest, the way it did when he knew he was doing something he shouldn’t.

The garden at the back was worse than the front.

Weeds up to his knees. A shed with its door hanging off. Rusted washing line posts with no line. The fence between number nine and the next property was broken in two places, and through one of the gaps he could see into the next garden — a trampoline, a dog kennel, a child’s plastic castle half-buried in leaves. Normal life, next door. And then number nine, pressing against it.

He tried the back door. Locked. Not a modern lock — an old Yale, and the wood around the frame was rotten. A determined kick would have taken it, but he wasn’t there yet.

Instead he stood and looked up.

The back bedroom window — the same one he’d seen the figure in — was directly above him. Cream curtain inside. No light showing. But something about the fabric didn’t move with the wind the way it should have. It held its shape, too rigid. As if someone was standing right behind it, looking down, pressing their weight into the hem.

He took a step back and almost slipped on a paving stone that was loose.

Then he heard it.

From inside the house. Very faint. A sound like a pipe dripping, but not water. Something thicker.

He stood there for a long time, listening, and the longer he listened the more he became aware that the silence wasn’t empty. It was held. Like a room full of people who had all gone still at the same moment because they heard someone approaching.

Callum went home without touching anything.

He lay in bed for hours after, staring at the ceiling, listening to Owen breathe through the wall. The drip sound followed him inside. It sat in his ears. It sat in his skull.

By four in the morning he was sure of one thing.

The house opposite was not abandoned.

And whatever was inside had not been hiding from him.

It had been waiting for him to come close enough to hear.
