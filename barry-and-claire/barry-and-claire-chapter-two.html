Barry and Claire

Chapter Two: The Bath and the Curtain

She was there when I came out of the bathroom. Not waiting — she would not wait, not exactly — but sitting up, the blanket pulled around her shoulders, the card in her hand.

“You’re back,” she said.

“I am.”

“Did you soak long enough?”

“Long enough.”

She smiled. She was easier now. The tightness around her eyes had softened. I think the bath had done something to her too, not because she was in it, but because she knew I was in it, warm and safe behind the door, and she had been the one to make it that way.

“I made more tea,” she said.

We sat at the table. The candle had burned to a nub. Outside, the rain was heavier. I could hear it on the roof of the launderette below, a steady hiss that made the flat feel smaller and safer at the same time.

“I have a boyfriend,” she said.

The words landed flat. Not painful — just flat. The way a stone lands in mud.

“Okay,” I said.

“His name is Dan. He’s… fine. He works in a warehouse. He plays football on Sundays. He leaves wet towels on the bathroom floor and forgets my birthday and thinks listening means waiting for his turn to speak.”

I said nothing. I listened.

“I don’t think I’m in love with him,” she said. “I don’t think I was ever really in love with him. I think I was just… glad to be chosen. And now that someone else has chosen me, even a little, I can’t pretend it’s enough.”

She said it plainly. No drama. No tears. Just the kind of truth that comes out in the hour after a bath, when the walls are down and the steam has cleared and you are sitting across from a man who has already bought you a cupcake and walked home in the rain.

“I don’t know what to do,” she said.

“You don’t have to do anything tonight.”

She nodded. She reached across the table. She found my hand. She held it. The cup of tea sat between us, warm and ordinary.

I started staying over on Tuesdays. Not every Tuesday. Some Tuesdays. Enough that the launderette staff began to nod at me when I came in. Enough that the crack in the bench in the park started to feel like ours instead of mine. Enough that I stopped counting the benches because I knew she would tell me when we had reached the oak.

The boyfriend — Dan — lasted three more weeks. She told me about it in fragments. He got angry about the text messages. He got angry about the cancelled plans. He got angry about the way she laughed at my jokes, which she said were not even very good.

“He said you were taking advantage,” she said.

“Of what?”

“The… I don’t know. The situation. The blindness. He said you were using it.”

I laughed. I could not help it. “I’m using it to what? Buy you cupcakes?”

She did not laugh. “I told him to leave.”

She told me this on a Thursday. We were at the oak. The dog was there. The spaniel was stealing sticks. She was quiet for a long time after she said it. I could hear the change in her breathing. Something had unclenched.

“Are you okay?” I asked.

“Yes,” she said. And she was.

A week later, she suggested swimming.

“There’s a pool near my flat,” she said. “It’s quiet in the mornings. We could go before work.”

“I don’t swim well,” I said.

“I know. I’ll help.”

I took my white walking stick — the one I use when the pavements are uneven, the one that folds into a bag when I need my hands free. We left my flat early. The journey was short but strange: her voice guiding me around a bin bag, her hand on my elbow turning me left at the corner, the sound of her keys in her pocket keeping time with my stick on the pavement. By the time we reached the pool, my heart was already beating faster than it had any right to.

The changing room was quiet. Empty, except for a cleaner pushing a mop in the far corner. We chose cubicles next to each other. There was a weird silence between us — not uncomfortable, exactly, but thick, the kind of silence that happens when two people are standing very close and not looking at each other and are aware, in every part of their bodies, that the other person is undressing nearby. I heard the rustle of her swimming costume. I heard the click of her locker. I heard her breathe out, steady, as though she was calming herself down.

When we were both changed, she knocked lightly on my partition wall.

“Ready?” she said.

“Yeah.”

We walked out together. The corridor was tiled and echoing. The air smelled of chlorine and feet. She took my arm. Not too tight. Just enough. Her skin was warm. Her wrist was bare. I could smell her perfume — something with jasmine in it, sweet but not heavy — rising from her neck as we walked. My heart hammered. I tried to match my breathing to the sound of her footsteps. I tried not to think about the cubicle, or the silence, or the fact that she was wearing a swimming costume and I was wearing one too and we were about to get into water together.

The pool was empty except for a lifeguard reading a magazine behind a glass screen. The water was blue and still. We stepped in at the shallow end. The cold took my breath away. Claire laughed — short, surprised, the sound I had come to know better than my own front door.

“It’s colder than I remembered,” she said.

We paddled. She told me to hold her shoulders. She guided me forward. I let my legs float. I let my arms drift. The water held me the way the bath had held me, but lighter, and with her perfume in the air around me, and with the sound of her voice saying, “You’re doing fine,” over and over.

After twenty minutes, we got out. We showered. We changed again. The cubicle silence was different this time — softer, as though the water had washed some of the nerves away. We walked back to her flat, damp and cold and hungry, and she made toast while I sat on the kitchen floor with a towel around my shoulders because I did not want to sit at the table while she was still in her towel.

We ate toast. We drank coffee. The flat smelled of chlorine and toast and her jasmine perfume, which had followed me from the pool and now lived in the towel around my shoulders.

“Barry,” she said, after a while.

“Yeah?”

“I think you should stop worrying about… you know. Being a virgin.”

The words landed differently than the boyfriend words. Not flat. Warm. Like a hand placed carefully on your chest.

“I’m thirty-eight,” I said.

“I know.”

“It feels late.”

“It’s only late if you’re timing it.”

I laughed. “Is that what you think?”

“I think you’re overthinking it. I think you’re waiting for it to be perfect, and it won’t be perfect, and that’s the point.”

I said nothing. The toast crunched. The kettle ticked as it cooled.

“If you want,” she said, “I could help you find someone.”

The words were quiet. She said them looking out of the window, not at me, like she was giving me the option of pretending she had not said them if I wanted.

“Help me find someone?” I repeated.

“Yeah. A girlfriend. Someone who wouldn’t… I don’t know. Someone who would get it.”

“Get what?”

“The blindness. The patience. The way you notice things other people miss. The way you remember the crack in the bench and the way I take my coffee and the sound my laugh makes before it really starts.”

I thought about Dan. I thought about the queue. I thought about the train. I thought about the bath, the curtain, the hour in the steam while she slept on the sofa.

“I think I’ve already found someone,” I said.

She turned. She looked at me. I could not see her face, but I could hear the shift in her breathing — the tiny intake, the pause, the slower release.

“Barry,” she said.

“Yeah?”

“That’s… that’s a big thing to say.”

“I know.”

“And you’re saying it after toast. With chlorine in your hair. While I’m still in a towel.”

“I’m saying it now,” I said. “Because I’m not timing it either.”

She was quiet for a long time. Then I heard her move. She knelt on the floor beside me. She smelled of chlorine and jasmine and coffee. She took my hand. She placed it on her face, just for a second, so I could feel the warmth of her cheek and the damp edge of her hair.

“I’m falling for you too,” she said.

And then she kissed me. It was quick. It was careful. It was the kind of kiss that says *I mean this* without saying anything at all.

When she pulled away, she laughed. The surprised laugh. The climbing one.

“There,” she said. “Now we’re even.”

“Even?” I asked.

“The cupcake. The bath. The pool. The toast.” She paused. “The virginity confession.”

I laughed. I could not help it. “You’re terrible.”

“I know.”

She rested her head on my shoulder. We sat on the kitchen floor and listened to the launderette hum below us. Outside, the rain had stopped. The street was ordinary. The flat was ordinary. But something had shifted, the way the atmosphere shifts before a storm — not in the sky, but in the air, in the way the light holds itself, in the way two people sit on a kitchen floor and know, without saying it, that they are no longer waiting.

“Do you want to stay?” she asked.

“I don’t have clothes.”

“You can wear the dressing gown. It’s too big for me anyway.”

I stayed. She found me a t-shirt with a coffee stain and jogging bottoms that were too long. We watched a film. I did not follow the plot. I was too aware of the weight of her arm against mine, too conscious of the way her perfume had followed me from the pool and now lived in the towel around my shoulders.

At bedtime, she led me to the bedroom. The duvet was the colour of wheat. She climbed in beside me. She lay very still, her back to my chest, and let me hold her the way you hold something that could blow away in the night.

“Goodnight, Barry,” she said.

“Goodnight, Claire.”

I fell asleep listening to her breathe. For the first time in six years, I was not listening for the next sound. I was not trying to place it. I was simply sharing it.

Outside, the launderette hummed. The flat settled.

And I thought: *this is it.*

*This is the thing the house was trying to tell me.*

*Not grief. Not fear.*

*This.*
