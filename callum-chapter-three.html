Chapter Three: The Noise From Upstairs

Owen found the screwdriver on Sunday.

He was rooting through the kitchen drawer looking for a battery for his remote-control car and came out with the flathead instead, holding it up like he’d discovered something dangerous.

“What’s this?”

Callum looked up from his laptop. “Put it back.”

“What’s it for?”

“Nothing.”

“Is it for opening things?”

“Yes. Put it back.”

Owen didn’t put it back. He sat at the table and turned it over in his fingers, the metal catching the light. He was quiet for a while, which was unusual. Owen processed things out loud; silence meant he’d gone somewhere complicated.

“The house opposite is weird,” he said eventually.

Callum’s fingers stopped on the keyboard. “What do you mean?”

“I saw someone.”

Callum turned. “When?”

“Yesterday. In the window.”

“Which window?”

“The top one. The one that looks at our kitchen.”

Callum stood up. He crossed to the window and looked out. Number nine was there, as always. Cream curtains. No movement. The garden looked slightly more overgrown than it had the day before, if that was possible.

“You saw someone?” he asked.

“Yeah. A man. He was looking at me.”

“Are you sure?”

“Yeah. He had a white face.”

Callum sat back down. The probate record came back into his head — M. H. Alderton, no next of kin. A dead man’s house, then. Or a house that belonged to a dead man and hadn’t been settled yet. That explained the lights going on at odd hours. Someone was sorting through things. Clearing out. Doing whatever people do when a house has been waiting for them to notice it.

“Mum said don’t look at it,” Owen added.

“Mum said a lot of things.”

“She said the house is sad.”

Callum didn’t answer. He went back to the laptop. The probate entry had a date — March, six years ago. Six years of legal limbo. Six years of a house sitting in the system while relatives, if there were any, ignored the letters.

He stood again and went to the drawer. Owen had put the screwdriver back. Callum took it out again, checked it was the same one, then closed the drawer without taking it.

Not yet.

Monday brought rain. Thick, grey, relentless. Callum watched it from his room and thought about the back door of number nine. The Yale. The rotten frame. The smell inside.

He tried calling Romano’s Pizza to ask about his final pay. The number went to a machine that told him the account was no longer active. He hung up and threw his phone on the bed.

Deborah came home late, wet through, and made pasta that boiled over because she’d gone to check Owen’s homework and forgotten about it. The kitchen filled with steam and the smell of burnt starch. She laughed about it afterwards, a short, sharp sound that wasn’t really amusement. She was tired. They were all tired.

After Owen went to bed, Callum went back to the laptop. He’d started digging into old local news archives, anything mentioning Alder Lane or number nine specifically. Found a single article from the local rag, five years old. House clearance postponed due to probate dispute. No names. No details.

He copied the URL into his notes.

Then he opened a new tab and searched for “houses watched from windows.”

Most results were about burglars. About keeping an eye on your property. About ring doorbells and neighbourhood watch. Nothing about the feeling of being observed by a building itself.

He closed the tab.

Rain lashed the window. The streetlight outside turned the wet pavement the colour of old pewter. Somewhere a siren went past, distant and brief, then silence again.

He stood and crossed to the curtain. Pulled it an inch aside.

Number nine was dark. The upstairs window showed nothing. But something about the darkness didn’t look natural. Too complete. Too deliberate. As if the house had closed its eyes.

He let the curtain fall.

In the kitchen below, Deborah moved quietly, putting things away. He could hear the scrape of a chair, the fridge door opening and closing. She was preparing for tomorrow the way she always did — laying out clothes, packing Owen’s bag, making sure the bills were in a pile by the letter tray.

He thought about the figure in the window. Not the one from last night — a different one. Taller. Still. The sort of person who stands in a room and makes the room feel smaller.

He wondered if the house had more than one.

He went to bed at midnight. Owen was already asleep, breathing in that shallow, snuffling way he had when he had a cold coming on. Callum lay on his back and listened to the pipes tick. The house was settling around him, walls breathing, timbers creaking as they cooled.

Outside, the rain slowed to a drizzle.

Somewhere down the lane a car door slammed. The house settled around him.

Callum closed his eyes.

Behind his eyelids he saw number nine. The cream curtains. The cracked path. The back door with the Yale that would open if you pressed it right.

And upstairs, in the front room, a shape standing very still, watching the boy across the road who had finally started looking back.
