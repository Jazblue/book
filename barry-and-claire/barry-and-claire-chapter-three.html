Barry and Claire

Chapter Three: The Wine and the Bath

She opened a bottle of wine on a Tuesday evening, and neither of us noticed the night getting later until it was too late to drive home.

The wine was cheap. I know that because she told me the price before she opened it — three pounds ninety-nine from the supermarket, something with a yellow label and a screw top. She poured two glasses. The kitchen was warm. The launderette was closed. The flat felt suspended in the particular quiet that comes after eight o’clock on a weeknight, when the street outside has slowed down and the only sounds are the boiler and the fridge and the occasional car passing with its headlights sweeping across the ceiling.

We sat on the kitchen floor. There was no table free — the surfaces were covered in books and papers and a half-finished jigsaw she had been doing on the coffee table. The floor was linoleum, cold at first, then warmer where we sat. The wine was white. It tasted of lemons and something a little like cardboard. I liked it.

“I don’t have to be up early tomorrow,” she said.

“Me neither,” I said. Which was not strictly true. I had a client at ten. But I did not say that. I wanted the night to last. I wanted the wine to keep tasting like lemons and cardboard and possibility.

We talked. Not about anything important. About the pool. About the way the lifeguard had read his magazine without looking up. About the café and the sticky table and the chips that had gone cold. About the crack in the bench and whether we should bring a cushion next time. About the dog that stole sticks and whether it was the same dog every week or whether there was a whole gang of them taking turns.

I do not remember who noticed the glasses were empty first. I think it was me. I held mine up to the light. It was clear. She laughed. She opened the bottle again. This time, the wine was colder. The kitchen was warmer. The street was quieter.

“I could get drunk,” she said.

“You are drunk,” I said.

“Not really. Just enough.”

I did not argue. I poured more. The yellow label. The screw top. The three pounds ninety-nine that was turning into the best evening I had had in years.

Later — I do not know how much later — she said she wanted a bath.

“Again?” I said.

“Again. It’s our thing now.”

I agreed. I always agreed when it came to baths. There was something about the steam and the curtain and the way the water made the flat feel like a ship at sea — not in danger, just moving, just somewhere else.

She ran it. The pipes ticked. The water filled. I undressed in the bedroom. I heard her moving in the bathroom — the tap running, the bottle of shampoo being opened, the lighter striking for the candle. I stood at the bedroom door and listened. The flat was very quiet. The wine was in my head, warm and golden. I felt loose in a way I had not felt since before the blindness, since before the careful routines, since before I started counting benches and measuring days in walks and silences and the sound of a laugh that started low and climbed.

“The curtain’s open,” she called.

I walked down the hall. The floorboards were cold under my feet. The bathroom light was warm. The door was ajar. I pushed it open.

The bath was full. The steam rose. She was already in it, up to her shoulders, her hair pinned up with a clip, her arms resting on the edge. The candle flickered on the windowsill. The curtain was drawn back, not closed, so the room was visible from the doorway — the sink, the mirror, the towel rail, the bath, her shoulders in the water.

It was eerie. Not frightening. Just… charged. The way a room feels when two people have crossed a line and both know it and neither is going back. The steam made the edges soft. The candle made the light warm. The water made the room sound different — every sound was muffled, wrapped, as though the bath had its own atmosphere, its own rules, its own way of holding time.

“Come in,” she said.

I did not close the door. I stood in the doorway and looked — not at her, because I could not see her properly, but at the scene as a whole, the way you look at a painting in the dark, trying to read the shapes. The bathroom was small. The bath was white. The candle was orange. She was a shape in the water, still, waiting.

I undressed. I folded my clothes on the closed lid of the toilet. I got in. The water was too hot. I sank down. She shifted. She made room. She did not speak. The silence between us was not the silence of the cubicle at the pool, thick and aware. It was softer. It was the silence of a room where two people are doing something ordinary together and have realised, at the same moment, that it is not ordinary at all.

I sat with my back against the side. She sat with her back against mine. I could feel her warmth through the water. I could smell her shampoo — coconut, I think, something sweet and clean. I could hear her breathing. Slow. Steady. Not nervous. Not excited. Just… present. The way you breathe when you have stopped running and have finally reached the place you were trying to get to.

“I’m glad you stayed,” she said.

“Me too.”

“I didn’t want the night to end.”

“I know.”

We sat for a long time. The candle burned down. The water cooled. The steam thinned. Somewhere above us, in the flat upstairs, we heard a sound. Not a footstep. Not a voice. A rhythm. A creak. The particular sound a bed makes when two people are moving together in the dark. It was faint at first, then clearer, then unmistakable. The ceiling transmitted it the way ceilings transmit everything — the footsteps, the arguments, the music, the lovemaking. This was the first time I had heard the upstairs couple. I had known they were there. I had heard their footsteps. I had heard their television. But this was different. This was intimate. This was the kind of sound that makes you aware, in a very direct way, that you are not alone in the building.

Claire stiffened. I felt it through her back against mine. She did not speak. She did not move. She just listened, the way you listen to something you cannot stop and do not want to acknowledge.

I said nothing. I did not have to. The sounds continued — the creak of the mattress, the shift of weight, the quiet sounds that people make when they are close and unselfconscious and have forgotten, or do not care, that the ceiling is thin and the neighbours are awake. I found myself listening closely. Not out of eavesdropping, exactly — though I will not pretend I was not listening — but out of curiosity. Out of a desire to understand the mechanics of something I had not participated in for a very long time. Out of the simple, human need to know what the thing looks like from the outside when you are standing in a bath with a woman who has just kissed you and said she is falling for you and has not yet asked you to leave.

I enjoyed it. I will not pretend otherwise. It was a performance for my ears — raw, unscripted, occasionally funny in its timing, occasionally tender in its pauses. I lay in the water and I listened and I thought: *this is what I have been missing.* Not the sex itself — not yet — but the proximity. The shared air. The sound of another person breathing in the same room and knowing they are breathing with you, not at you.

When it stopped, the flat went very quiet. The launderette hummed. The pipes ticked. The ceiling settled. Claire let out a breath. I felt it through her back.

“Too much information?” she said.

I laughed. “A bit.”

She turned in the water. She shifted until she was facing me. The candle was between us. The steam was thinning. I could hear the outline of her now — the small movements of her shoulders, the shift of her weight, the smile rising in her voice like she was letting go of something she had been holding since the pool, since the café, since the first kiss on the kitchen floor.

“They’re always at it,” she said. “Every Thursday. Like clockwork.”

“Do they know we can hear?”

“I think they do. I think they don’t care.”

I thought about that. A flat above a launderette, thin walls, a ceiling that carried every sound, two people making love at ten o’clock on a Tuesday with no shame and no curtains and no consideration for the forty-year-old blind man in the bath below who had not had sex in eight years and was listening with a attention that bordered on reverence.

“I don’t mind,” I said.

“I know,” she said. “That’s the thing. I knew you wouldn’t mind. You listen to everything. The park. The dogs. The seal documentary. You notice things other people miss. I knew you’d listen to them too.”

She reached for the tap. She let the water out. The gurgle was loud in the quiet flat. She stood up. She wrapped a towel around herself. She stepped out. I stayed sitting. The water was cooling. The steam was gone. The candle had burned to a nub.

“You can sleep in my bed,” she said. “But I don’t want the lumpy old couch. I’ve had it for three years and it’s given me back pain every morning since. You can’t sleep on it.”

“I wasn’t going to ask,” I said.

“I know. But I’m telling you anyway. The couch is out. The bed is in. Top to tail.”

“Top to tail?”

“Back to back. Feet to head. It’s the only way we both fit without me kicking you in the ribs all night.”

I laughed. “Deal.”

She dried herself. She gave me a towel. I dried myself. I followed her down the hall to the bedroom. The duvet was the colour of wheat. The pillow smelled of her shampoo. She climbed in on the left side. I climbed in on the right. She turned away from me. I turned away from her. Our backs were together. Our feet were at opposite ends of the bed.

It was strange. I had never slept this way with anyone. Even as a child, I had always wanted to be facing someone, or at least close enough to see their face. But there was something gentle about it, something considerate — the acknowledgement that we were still finding each other, still learning the shape of each other’s bodies, and that sometimes the kindest thing you can do is give someone space while you are in the same bed.

“Goodnight, Barry,” she said.

“Goodnight, Claire.”

I lay there. I felt the mattress give where she lay. I heard her breathing. I thought about the upstairs couple. I thought about the wine. I thought about the bath and the curtain and the way the steam had thinned and the way she had said, *Too much information?* like she was checking whether I was still on her side of the line, and I had said *A bit* and she had laughed and turned to face me and for a second I had seen the outline of her face in the candlelight, and she had looked like someone who had been waiting for permission to stop being careful.

The launderette hummed. The flat settled. Somewhere in the distance, a train went past. I thought about the Crack in the bench in the park. I thought about the queue. I thought about the train. I thought about the bath, the curtain, the pool, the café, the kiss on the kitchen floor.

And I thought, with a quiet that was deeper than any silence I had known before: *this is the beginning.*

*Not the end. The beginning.*

*And it is not perfect. It is cheap wine and lumpy couches and upstairs neighbours who make love at ten o’clock and a bath with no curtain and a girl who bites her lip when she is thinking and laughs like a song building before the chorus.*

*But it is mine.*

*And it is hers.*

*And for the first time in a long time, that is enough.*
