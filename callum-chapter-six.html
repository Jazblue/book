Chapter Six: The Threshold Within

Callum borrowed his mother’s screwdriver on Tuesday evening.

He waited until she was in the living room watching television with Owen, then slipped into the kitchen drawer and palmed it. Small flathead. Good condition. The sort of tool that could open a back door if you knew how to use it.

He went straight to number nine.

This time there was no hesitation. He crossed the road with purpose, the screwdriver heavy in his pocket, and stood at the back gate. The garden looked the same — weeds, broken shed, the neighbour’s trampoline visible through the fence gap. The air smelled of rain and something else. Something warm. Almost sweet, but wrong, like fruit left too long in a bowl.

He tried the back door again.

The Yale was still swollen. He knelt and put the screwdriver into the gap beside the lock, feeling for the catch. It was stiff. He pressed harder. The screwdriver slipped and scratched the paint.

He swore quietly.

Tried again. Angled the blade differently. Pressed. The lock gave — a soft click, almost disappointed — and the door opened inward.

The smell hit him first.

Damp, yes. But also earth, and something older. A house that had breathed the same air for decades and never let it out.

He stepped inside.

The back porch was small. Linoleum curling at the edges. A coat rack with one hook left, a waxed jacket hanging on it, size large, collar turned up. The kitchen door stood ajar. Through the gap he could see a dark room beyond, counters cluttered with things he couldn’t make out.

He moved forward.

The kitchen was colder than the porch. The window above the sink was painted shut, as he’d seen from outside. The counters held jars, a kettle, a cutting board with something dried on it that might have been bread. The fridge hummed — that was the sound he’d heard from outside.

He opened the fridge.

Light spilled out. Inside: milk, gone off. Bread, moulding at the edges. A jar of something pickled, lid rusted shut. A carton of orange juice, empty, standing in the door shelf like it had been put there and forgotten.

He closed it.

The hum filled the silence again.

He went through to the living room. Sofa covered in a sheet. Television in the corner, screen dark. Coffee table with a single mug, two rings of tea stain on the wood, as if someone had set it down and never picked it up again.

The stairs creaked under his weight.

Upstairs was a carpeted landing, the stairs runner worn through in the centre to the colour of dirt. Two doors off the landing: left and right. He tried the left first.

A bedroom. Double bed with no duvet, just a mattress and a single grey blanket. Wardrobe open, empty except for one coat on a hanger. A mirror on the wall with black-edged corners. On the bedside table: a lamp with no bulb, a book with no title on the spine, a glass of water that had dried to a ring in the wood.

He stood in the doorway and let the room look at him.

It was the most ordinary room he’d ever been in. And that was what made it wrong. Not blood. Not occult symbols. Not a message scrawled on the wall. Just a bed, a coat, a glass of water. The detritus of someone who had lived here briefly and then stopped.

He closed the door and tried the right.

Bathroom. Mould in the shower. A toothbrush in a mug. Towel on the floor, damp.

He thought of Owen’s Spider-Man flannel at home, hanging over the bathroom rail. The same domestic disorder, but there it had context. Here it didn’t. Here it looked like evidence.

Back on the landing, the door at the end — the one that looked over the front of the house.

Callum stood before it.

He could have opened it. He could have stood where the figure had stood and looked out at Alder Lane, at his own house, at his own window. But something held him back. Not fear, exactly. More like respect. Or politeness. As if the room was occupied, even now, by something that didn’t want him seeing it from the other side.

He went downstairs instead.

The kitchen again. Then the back door. He stepped outside and stood in the garden, breathing the damp air, listening to the house breathe behind him.

A noise from upstairs.

Very faint. A floorboard.

He looked up at the back bedroom window. The curtain moved — not much. Not dramatically. Just enough to tell him he wasn’t alone in there.

He didn’t run.

He closed the back door gently. Locked it from the outside, or tried to — the lock was so rotten it turned without catching. He left it as he’d found it.

Crossed the road. Got into his house through the back door. Climbed the stairs quietly. Owen snored softly through the wall.

Callum lay down and pulled the duvet up.

He didn’t sleep for a long time. When he did, he dreamt of the bedroom at the top of the stairs. In the dream, the coat on the hanger turned around. The glass of water filled itself.

And the figure in the window was not looking at the street.

It was looking at him.
