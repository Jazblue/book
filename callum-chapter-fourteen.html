Chapter Fourteen: The Voice in the Walls

Megan heard it first on Friday night, from the bottom of the stairs.

She had gone down to get water. The tap in the upstairs bathroom had been making a sound she could not identify — a gurgle, then a silence, then a gurgle again, as though something was moving through the pipes that was not water. She had stood at the sink in the dark for a while listening to it, then given up and gone downstairs to the kitchen tap instead.

She was filling a glass when she heard it.

A voice. Very quiet. Coming from somewhere in the walls.

Not the sound of a radio from next door. Not the murmur of a television through a brick wall. This was inside the house. Inside the walls. The kind of voice that might have been speaking in another room — another house, even — and had travelled through the plaster and the lath and the insulation and arrived at the kitchen as nothing more than a murmur. But it was a murmur she could almost understand. The words were just below the threshold. If she leaned closer, she could feel the shape of them in her ear, the way you feel the shape of a word someone is saying in a dream before you wake up and cannot remember it.

Megan set the glass down. She stood in the dark kitchen and listened.

The voice came again. A man’s voice, she thought. Low. Steady. Speaking slowly, as though to someone who was not paying attention. The words were still not clear but the tone was — patient. Instruction. The way a person speaks when they are teaching something to a child, or reminding someone of a task they have been asked to do many times before.

She walked to the wall. Pressed her ear against the plaster.

Nothing. Just the house. The pipes. The faint, distant sound of traffic on the road.

But when she stepped back, she heard it again. Louder this time. Or closer. It seemed to be coming from the hallway now, from the wall beside the front door.

Megan stood in the kitchen and listened to number nine speak to itself.

She did not sleep that night. She sat on the stairs — halfway up, where she could see both the ground floor and the landing and the door to the locked room at the end — and she waited. The cold came first. Not the cold of a house with the heating off — she had lived in flats with no heating, had slept in stairwells and bus shelters and once in a phone box in Rochester when it had rained for three days straight. This was different. It was a cold that started in her bones and moved outward, that made her teeth ache and her fingers stiffen and her breath come out in a visible cloud even though the stairs were not that cold.

It was coldest near the locked room.

Megan sat as far from that door as she could manage and still see it. The breathing had stopped — she had not heard it since the night before — but the silence was worse. The silence was not empty. The silence was holding something. Waiting.

She saw the shadow at around three in the morning.

It was at the far end of the landing, near the room she had not opened. Not a person — she told herself that firmly, and believed it, mostly — but a shadow the shape of a person. Tall. Standing very still. Watching the staircase.

Megan did not move. She did not breathe. She let her eyes adjust and the shadow adjusted with them, shifting slightly when she shifted, always at the edge of what she could see clearly, never quite resolving into something solid.

It stayed for a long time. Then it was gone.

Megan sat on the stairs until dawn. When the grey light came through the hall window, she stood up and went to the kitchen and made tea and drank it so quickly it scalded her tongue and she did not notice.

She needed to tell someone. She had known this for a while — since the breathing, since the footsteps, since the handle turning — but she had not done it. Telling meant involving someone else. Telling meant making it real. But she was not sure how much longer she could be the only person who knew what was happening in number nine.

She thought about the girl in the flat she had shared in Colchester, the one who had listened when Daniel shouted and had called the police when the walls shook. That girl had been brave. Megan had not been brave. Megan had run. Running was what she did. It was the only skill she had ever truly mastered.

But she could not run from this. Not because it was impossible to run from a house — houses could be left, doors could be locked, keys could be thrown away — but because the thing in the walls was not contained to number nine. It had followed her to the gate on Tuesday night. It had been in the shed on Wednesday. It had breathed in the garden while she stood at the front door with the key in her pocket. It was already outside.

That was the thought that made her cross the road.

She did not plan it. She did not put on shoes or find a coat or check her reflection in the hallway mirror. She walked out of number nine in her socks and her t-shirt and the cold morning air and she crossed the road and she stood on the pavement outside number eleven and she looked up at the window on the first floor where she had seen the boy standing night after night.

He was there now. Standing at the glass. Watching her.

Megan raised one hand. Very slightly. A wave. A signal. I know you’re there. I know you’ve been watching. I need to tell you something.

He did not wave back. He did not move. He simply looked at her the way people look at things they have imagined finally appearing in front of them.

Megan stood on the pavement. Her toes were cold against the tarmac. The street was empty except for a cat that moved along the wall at number eight with its back arched and its tail straight. She waited to see if the boy would open the window. He did not. After a while he turned away and the curtain shifted and he was gone.

Megan stood for another minute. Then she went home.

She did not sleep again that day. She sat at the kitchen table with the dented key in front of her and she thought about the voice in the walls and the shadow on the landing and the cold that had come from nowhere and settled in her bones. She thought about the boy across the road — Callum, she had learned his name from the postman, who had mentioned it casually as he handed her the parcel on Tuesday: he’s a quiet one, that one. Lives with his mum and little brother. Keeps himself to himself mostly. She had said nothing. She had taken the parcel and closed the door and stood in the hall listening to the house breathe.

Callum.

She said the name out loud. It sounded small in the kitchen. Small and careful, the way names sound when you have not said them before and are not sure how they fit in your mouth.

That evening, she stood at the window with the curtain pulled back and waited for him. He came at the usual time — she had learned his rhythm now, the way people learn the rhythms of things they watch. Eight o’clock. The streetlights came on. The television in the living room of number eleven flickered on and then off, the way it did when Deborah was telling Owen to turn it off and get ready for bed. Then the landing light went on. Then the first-floor light. Then Callum appeared at the window.

He saw her immediately. Not the way people see things they expect to see — the way they glance at the postbox or the bin or the neighbour’s cat. He saw her the way you see something that has been missing and has suddenly reappeared. His head moved. His eyes found her. He stood very still.

Megan raised her hand again. She kept it raised. A question. Are you there? Are you seeing this?

He did not raise his hand back. But he did not look away. He stood at the window and he looked at her and she felt something shift between them — across the road, across the pavement, across the gap that separated two people who had been watching the same haunted house from opposite sides of the street. Not trust. Not yet. But recognition. The recognition of two people who have seen the same impossible thing and are still trying to decide whether to run or stay.

Megan lowered her hand. She held up a finger instead. One finger. A signal. Wait.

Then she pointed to the door of number nine — the front door, visible from the pavement, the one with the missing letter flap. Then she pointed upstairs. Then she placed her hand flat against her chest and held it there.

Something in this house. Inside me. Inside the walls.

Callum watched her. The curtain behind him moved. He did not seem to notice.

Megan turned away from the window and walked to the centre of the room. She stood in the empty space where the carpet had been pulled up in one corner — she had found it rolled there on her first day, as though someone had started to lift it and then stopped — and she spoke to the ceiling. Not loudly. Not a shout. A voice. The same kind of voice she had heard in the walls the night before.

“I know you’re there,” she said.

The house was quiet.

“I’m not leaving. If you want me to leave, you’ll have to do better than that.”

Still quiet. But the cold had moved. It was no longer coming from upstairs. It was coming from the room behind her — the kitchen, where the tap was still dripping, where the tin of baked beans sat on the counter with the spoon in it. She turned slowly.

The kitchen doorway was empty. The hall beyond it was empty. But the cold was there. She could feel it on the back of her neck, the way you feel a presence in a room you have entered without realising someone else is already there.

She walked back to the window. Callum was still there.

She pointed to her chest again. Then she pointed to the upstairs window of number nine — the one with the curtain that moved. Then she pointed across the road at him.

You see it too.

Callum nodded.

Megan let out a breath she had not realised she was holding. The cold in the room seemed to deepen, as though the house had noticed the exchange and was responding to it — not with hostility, not exactly, but with attention. The way an animal watches a prey animal that has realised it is being watched.

She stood at the window and she looked at Callum and she did not look away. The house was cold behind her. The voice in the walls was quiet now, but it had not gone. It was waiting. Listening. Learning their names the way Megan had learned his.

Somewhere in the dark of number nine, a door closed. Not the front door — the back door. The one Megan had left unlocked when she crossed the road. She had not locked it behind her. She had forgotten.

She looked at Callum. He was frowning now, his brow tight, his eyes moving from her face to something behind her, to the house she was standing in.

He had seen the curtain move.

Or he had seen something else. Something that made the skin on his arms prickle and his throat go dry and his instinct — the same instinct that had made him stand at his window for weeks watching a house he should have left alone — scream at him to go.

Megan did not go.

She stood at the window and she let the cold come. She let the house look at her. She let the voice in the walls hum its patient, unplaceable song.

She was not alone anymore.

And that, she thought, was both the bravest thing she had ever done and the stupidest.
