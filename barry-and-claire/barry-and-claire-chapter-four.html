Barry and Claire

Chapter Four: The Dressing Gown

She picked a film with a love scene in it. She wanted me to hear it. She wanted to tell me what it looked like.

It was a Thursday. Dan had moved out the week before. The flat was different without his things in it — not emptier, exactly, but cleaner. Quieter. The air had changed. The hallway did not smell of football boots and whatever cheap body wash he used. The kitchen surfaces were clear. The bathroom sink was not clogged with a single grey hair.

Claire had taken the afternoon off. She was supposed to be doing a volunteer shift — four hours at the hospital, the kind of thing she put on her CV — but she texted me at midday and said, *Sick of hospitals. Coming round yours.*

I was waiting. I had bought hummus. I had bought carrots. I had bought the wine again, the same three-pound ninety-nine yellow-label stuff, because she had liked it and I did not know any better.

She arrived at one. She let herself in. She dropped her bag on the floor. She made a sound that was half sigh, half laugh, like she was finally somewhere she did not have to perform.

“Dan’s gone,” she said, as if I did not know.

“I heard.”

“He left a box of CDs. I threw them out.”

“Good.”

She laughed. She found the wine. She found two glasses. We sat on my sofa — lumpy, old, the one I had owned since my twenties — and she picked a film. I did not know what it was. I could not see the cover. I only knew it was a romance, or a drama, or something that had been described to me as *beautiful* and *devastating* and *not suitable for Tuesday afternoons*, the last of which had made me want to watch it immediately.

She put it on. She snuggled against me. I put my arm around her. The dressing gown I was wearing was old — blue, faded, the belt missing — and I had not bothered to tie it. It hung open. Underneath I was wearing boxer shorts, old, with a coffee stain on the leg. I was not thinking about my body. I was thinking about the weight of her head on my shoulder, the smell of her hair, the way her breathing slowed when she was content.

Twenty minutes in, I realised it was not the sort of film that had a love scene in it. It was the sort of film that was almost entirely made of love scenes, if you counted longing looks and hands almost touching and conversations that meant more than the people having them realised.

Then the explicit one came on. Not pornographic. Just… honest. A man and a woman in a bedroom, the camera slow, the acting quiet, the kind of scene that makes you forget there is a camera at all.

I felt Claire shift against me. I felt the change in her breathing — slower, deeper, the way it changes when you are watching something that reminds your body of things your mind had not yet asked for.

“Tell me,” I said.

She was quiet for a moment. The film continued, and she described it in fragments — the light through curtains, the way the actors held each other, the quiet honesty of the camera. She spoke without detail, but her voice changed as she spoke. Lower. Softer. The room felt smaller, warmer, the space between us filled with something I could not name but could feel pressing against my ribs.

“It makes you think,” she said finally, and then she did not say anything else for a long time.

I listened to her breathing. I felt her warm against my side. I felt the dressing gown open against my chest, the air cool on my skin. I did not care. I was aware of my body in a way I had not been for a long time — not with shame, exactly, but with curiosity. With a desire to be near the woman who was sitting so close that the silence between us felt like a third person in the room.

I turned my head. I found her mouth, but she turned away before our lips met. She laughed, quietly, and rested her cheek against my shoulder instead. The moment passed, not into disappointment, but into something softer — the kind of intimacy that does not need to close the distance to be real.

When I pulled away, she was breathing fast. Her hand was on my chest. Her fingers were tracing the line of my sternum, not lower, but steady, like she was memorising the shape of him.

“I want this,” she said.

“I know.”

“Not just… I want this. Tonight. With you.”

The words were quiet. She said them looking at me, I could tell, though I could not see her face. I could hear the shift in her breathing. I could feel the warmth of her hand through the fabric.

And then, before I could answer, before I could say the thing I had been thinking since the pool, since the bath, since the moment on the kitchen floor — she said something else.

“I had a thought,” she said.

“What thought?”

She was quiet for a long time. The film played. The lovers moved. The camera held them.

“Dan,” she said.

My chest tightened. “What about him?”

“He asked if he could stay.”

The words landed like a door slamming. Not frightening — just sudden. Unlocked. Opened onto a room I had not known existed.

“Stay?” I repeated.

“Before he moved out. He said… he said he wanted to try one more time. He said he wanted to see if we could make it work, if he could just… be different. If I could just… meet him halfway.”

I said nothing. The dressing gown was open. The boxers were thin. The film continued. I felt the heat of her leg against mine. I felt the weight of her hand on my stomach. I felt the enormity of what she was saying, and the strangeness of it, and the way it made me feel — not jealous, not exactly, but… observed. Like my body was not my own but was already being watched by a ghost of a man who had left and wanted one last look at the woman he had failed to please.

“Did you say yes?” I asked.

“No. Not then. But I thought about it. After he left. I lay in bed and I thought about it and I was… I was awake. Not because of him. Because of the thought. Because of the image. Him staying. Me staying. The two of them combined in my head.”

She paused. She shifted. Her hand moved, just a little, but it did not go lower. It rested, steady, on my stomach.

“I’m not going back to him,” she said. “The thought was just… a thought. A way of understanding what I’d lost. What I’d accepted.”

The words hung in the air. The film played. The lovers held each other quietly, without rush, without shame. I thought about Dan in his new flat, in his box of CDs thrown out, in his warehouse job and his football Sundays. I thought about the way he had looked at Claire when he left — not angry, not sad, just… resigned. Like he had known, all along, that he was not the man she needed.

“If it pleases me,” she said, “I might do it. But only if it pleases me. Not for him. For me.”

She turned. She faced me. I could feel her breath on my face. I could feel the heat of her body. I could feel the truth of her words, and the control in them, and the way she had taken something that might have been degrading and turned it into something that was entirely hers.

“Do you want that?” she asked.

I thought about it. I thought about the upstairs couple. I thought about the bath and the curtain and the way the flat carried sound. I thought about the film, and her voice describing it, and the way she had said *I get wet like that* and *I never came with him* and *if it pleases me*. I thought about being watched. About being seen. About the specific, sharp clarity of knowing that someone is watching you touch the person you love and that the watching is making the touching better, not worse.

“If it pleases you,” I said.

She smiled. I could hear it in her voice. “That’s the right answer.”

She kissed me then, lightly, on the cheek. It was not the hungry kiss I had expected. It was quieter. Deliberate. The kind of kiss that says *I am choosing this* and *I am choosing you* and *I am choosing the way we do this*, all at once, but at her pace, not mine.

The film ended. We did not watch the credits. She turned it off. The flat went dark, except for the light from the streetlamp through the curtains, orange and thin. The dressing gown was still open. The boxers were still thin. The night was still there.

She led me to the bedroom. She undressed me slowly. She did not rush. She touched every part of me like she was memorising it — the callus on my thumb from the cane, the scar on my hip from when I was a child and fell through a glass door, the warmth of my skin, the shape of my chest, the way my breath hitched when she traced the line of my rib. She made me take off the boxers. She held them in her hands for a moment, as though reading the coffee stain like a map. When I was naked, she stood back. I could not see myself. But she touched my face, my shoulders, my back, and I knew she was seeing me the way I could not — entirely, without the blind spots I carried.

“You're beautiful,” she said.

“I know,” I said. “You're describing me.”

“I can feel you. I see you with my hands. I see you with my mouth. I see you with the way you listen to films and the way you carry the wine and the way you say *if it pleases you* like you mean it, like you are not just saying it but feeling it, every word.”

She climbed in beside me. She faced me. She put her hand on my face. She kissed me — slow, deep, the kind of kiss that is not a prelude but a destination. Her hand rested on my chest. She touched me gently, like a question she was asking with her fingertips. I gasped. She smiled against my mouth.

“I'm wet,” she said.

“I know.”

“For you.”

She guided me inside her. It was slow. It was warm. It was the most natural thing I had ever done, and the most terrifying, and the most right. She breathed. I breathed. The flat settled around us. The launderette hummed. Somewhere, a train went past. The bed creaked — the same sound the upstairs bed made, but softer, kinder, ours.

When she came, it was not faint. It was not the kind of climax you have when you are trying to prove something. It was loud, for her — quiet, for the flat, but loud for her — and her hands gripped my shoulders and her mouth was open and her breathing stopped and started and stopped again, and I felt it through her whole body, the way you feel a bell after it has been struck.

“Oh,” she said. “Oh. Oh.”

And then she was still. She lay beside me. Her head on my chest. Her hair in my mouth. Her breathing slowing. The warmth of her on my skin.

“There,” she said, into my chest. “That's what I meant.”

I held her. I did not move. I did not need to. The dressing gown was on the floor. The boxers were on the floor. The film was off. Dan was gone. The upstairs couple was quiet. The night was ours.

She fell asleep on my chest. I stayed awake. I listened to her breathe. I thought about the bench in the park. I thought about the queue. I thought about the train. I thought about the bath and the curtain and the pool and the café and the kiss on the kitchen floor and the film and the dressing gown and the words *I get wet like that* and *I never came with him* and *if it pleases me*.

And I thought: *this is the thing.*

*This is the thing the house was trying to tell me.*

*Not grief. Not fear. Not even love, exactly.*

*This.*

*The body knowing. The word yes. The woman on your chest who has just taught you what it means to be chosen, not out of obligation, not out of gladness to be chosen, but out of desire — pure, uncomplicated, unscripted desire.*

*She fell out of love with him,* I thought. *She fell into this.*

*And so did I.*

Outside, the streetlamp hummed. The train went past. The flat settled.

I was not a virgin anymore.

I was not waiting anymore.

I was home.
