Chapter Twenty-One: The Morning After

The front door opened onto a morning that had been waiting for him all night.

The letterbox was cold against his cheek. Callum knelt on the doormat and pushed it open slowly, the way you open a door into a room you know has been occupied while you were away. The smell of the street came in: wet tarmac, fried breakfast from the takeaway three doors down, and something else — something green and alive, as though the pavement itself had been growing overnight.

He stood up. He looked out.

Alder Lane was exactly as he had left it. Number eleven had its porch light still on, though the bulb was dying and the light was more orange than white. Number nine was there, the curtains drawn, the porch step damp with dew. The streetlamp at the corner hummed, a little louder than it usually did, as though it had been holding its breath too.

Behind him, the house was quiet. Not the house-through-the-wall quiet of the passage. The ordinary quiet of a house at six in the morning, when the only sounds are the fridge and the boiler and the settling of timber that has been cooling since midnight.

Callum closed the door. He locked it. The lock turned easily, the way locks do when they have been used properly and not tampered with by whatever had turned it the night before. He took the key from his pocket — the key from under his pillow, the ordinary house key — and put it back where it belonged. The other key, the one to number nine, he kept in his jeans pocket.

Deborah was in the kitchen. She was standing at the sink with a mug in her hand, staring out of the window at the garden as though she had not moved since he left. Her hair was still not brushed. Her dressing gown was the blue one with the burn mark on the cuff from the time she had tried to make toast and forgotten about it. She looked, Callum thought, like a woman who had been up all night thinking about things she could no longer keep inside her head.

“I told him,” she said.

Callum closed the kitchen door behind him. “Owen?”

Deborah nodded. She did not turn around. “He didn’t believe me. Not at first. Then he asked a question that made me wish he still didn’t.”

“What question?”

“He asked if Niall was the man in the kitchen.”

The house settled around them. The fridge hummed. Outside, a car started and drove away. Callum sat at the table and put the key on the wood between them. It made a small, hard sound.

“I went to number nine,” he said.

Deborah turned. Her eyes were red, but she had not been crying. She had been awake, that was all. Awake long enough for the redness to settle in the corners and not leave.

“I know,” she said. “The house told me.”

“What did it tell you?”

She laughed. It was a short, sharp sound, the kind of laugh that comes from surprise rather than humour. “It told me the same thing it always told me. That I had been keeping a door shut for nineteen years and wondering why the room behind it smelled of dust.”

Callum looked at the photograph she had left on the table the night before. The man in it was looking at the camera with an expression that Callum now recognised: not love, not exactly, but the sort of attention a person gives to something they know they are going to lose. A goodbye look.

“Owen wants to meet him,” Deborah said.

Callum’s stomach turned. “Who?”

“Ron. The plumber.”

He had forgotten about Ron. In the rush of the night before — the door, the wall, the room, the chair, the coat that was not a coat — the plumber had become a small thing, a detail in a story that had grown too big for its walls.

“He found the note,” Deborah said. “In the drawer. He’s been looking for him for months. Said he wanted to thank him. Said he thought Ron might like to know he had a son.”

Callum did not know what to say. The house breathed. The kettle clicked. Through the window, the garden looked ordinary — the shed at the bottom, the washing line, the bush that never flowered. But it was not ordinary. It was ordinary the way a face is ordinary when you have known it so long that you no longer see the lines in it.

“I need to go back,” Callum said.

“To number nine?”

“Tonight. After school.”

Deborah nodded. She picked up the mug and drank from it, and for the first time since he had come downstairs Callum thought she looked less like a woman carrying a secret and more like a woman who had just put one down.

“I’ll come with you,” she said.

Callum shook his head. “No. You need to be here when Owen gets home. You need to tell him everything. Not the ghost parts. The human parts.”

She thought about this. She set the mug down. “The human parts are the hardest parts.”

“I know.”

Outside, the postwoman walked past. The letterbox rattled. A catalogue landed on the mat. The ordinary world was reasserting itself, stubbornly, like a dog that has been told to stay off the sofa and has decided to wait until you look away.

Deborah walked to the back door and looked out at the garden. “I kept the note,” she said. “Ron’s note. It’s in the drawer with the microwave manual. I should have thrown it out. I should have thrown a lot of things out.”

Callum looked at the key. “Maybe it’s time.”

She smiled. It was a real smile, not the broken one from the night before. “Maybe it is.”

The sun came up properly then, the way it does in Kent in September — not a dramatic sunrise, just a slow brightening, the grey turning to gold, the dew on the shed roof catching the light. The street woke up. A car went past. A dog barked. Number nine stood at the end of the lane with its curtains drawn, patient and unhurried, the way houses are when they know they have all the time in the world.

Callum went upstairs to get ready for school. He put the spare key — the one to number nine — in his pocket. He did not lock his door.

For the first time in months, the house did not feel like it was watching him. It felt like it was waiting for him to come back.
