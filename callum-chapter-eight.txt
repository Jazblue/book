Chapter Eight: The Shed at the Bottom of the Garden

The shed had been there since they moved in, but Callum had never gone inside it. Deborah kept a padlock on the door, a small silver one that looked cheap but held fast. She used it for the lawnmower, she said, and the old paint tins, and things that Owen might hurt himself with.

Callum found the key on Sunday morning.

Deborah had been drying dishes at the sink, talking about her sister in Carlisle, about the traffic on the M6, about a neighbour who had parked too close to the drive. She set her ring down on the windowsill while she wiped her hands, and Callum saw it — a small silver key on a red fob, identical to the one on the shed.

He waited until she went to the toilet. Then he picked up the ring, took the key, and put it back.

The shed was at the bottom of the garden, behind the washing line that never saw use. The wood of the door was grey with age, and one panel was split near the bottom. The padlock opened with a click that sounded enormous in the quiet morning.

Inside, it smelled of grass cuttings and something chemical. A lawnmower with flat tyres. Two paint tins, lids rusted. A stack of old blankets in the corner, stiff with dust. A single chair, wooden, with a broken leg propped on a brick.

Callum closed the door behind him and locked it from the inside.

The light came through the crack in the panel and the gap under the door. It was enough. He sat on the chair and felt the wood press against his legs. No one could open this door without the key. No one could walk in without him knowing.

He stayed for an hour. He thought about the bathroom at home, about the lock he had installed, about Deborah turning the handle and expecting it to yield. Here, the door was his. Here, he was the one who decided.

When he heard Deborah calling his name from the house, he unlocked the door and stepped out. The air felt different in the garden. Colder. Sharper. He looked across the road at number nine, at the upstairs window where the curtain had moved, and for a moment he thought he saw a shape behind the glass. Not the figure. Something else. Something smaller. Something that might have been watching him back.

He went inside. Deborah was standing in the hallway with her hands on her hips.

“Where have you been?” she said.

“Garden,” Callum said.

“You’re getting grass on the carpet.”

“I took my shoes off.”

Deborah looked at his feet. They were clean. She looked at him for a long time, then turned away. “Dinner’s in ten minutes,” she said. “Wash your hands.”

Callum went upstairs to the bathroom. He locked the door. He ran the water until it was warm, and he washed his hands slowly, watching the steam fog the mirror. Through the wall, Owen was shouting at the television.

The shed at the bottom of the garden was small and cold and full of dust. But it was his. And for now, that had to be enough.
